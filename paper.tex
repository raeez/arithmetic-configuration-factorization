\begin{abstract}
For integral shifts, the allowed residue classes factor over coprime moduli.
Their normalized densities form a positive convergent singular product
when every prime leaves an allowed class.  A finite divisor-incidence map gives
a Rayleigh quotient that detects simultaneous prime translates.
We compare this quotient with coordinate integration on a Lebesgue simplex.
In every finite dimension $k\geq2$, the simplex operator has essential
spectrum $[0,M_{k-1}]$, where $M_j$ is its maximal Rayleigh value in
dimension $j$, and $M_k>M_{k-1}$.  Its maximal eigenvalue is simple;
normalized polynomial iterates converge in $L^2$ to its positive symmetric
unit eigenfunction.  Five explicit quadratic polynomials give rational
lower bounds in dimensions eight through twelve.
Under a stated level of distribution, the arithmetic forms converge
uniformly on each fixed smooth finite-dimensional subspace.
A sufficiently slow dense exhaustion gives strong operator convergence
and convergence of the maximal Rayleigh values, but no operator norm
convergence in dimensions at least two.
The incidence values also define exact smooth tests for the Fourier
boundary of the logarithmic derivative of the zeta function.
In one dimension, cell averages reconstruct the full coefficient quotient
and extend its prime pairing to the simplex Hilbert space.
Uniform full-quotient reconstruction in higher dimensions remains open.
\end{abstract}

A set of shifts is admissible when, for every prime, some residue class
makes all translates coprime to that prime.  Such a set supplies residue
classes on which every translate avoids prescribed small prime divisors.
Sieve coefficients then
assign a nonnegative weight to each integer in those classes.  The ratio of
the prime-weighted sum to the total weight detects prime translates.
To compare this finite ratio with an integral variational problem, one must
specify both the coefficient map and the topology of convergence.

The comparison uses smooth functions on a simplex.  Their logarithmic
samples determine the sieve coefficients by an invertible finite transform.
The arithmetic estimates control the resulting forms in the $C^1$ norm.
On finite-dimensional subspaces this becomes control relative to the
$L^2$ norm, which permits spectral comparison.  Point sampling itself has
a kernel on every smooth infinite-dimensional space.  This distinction
determines the range of the limiting theorem.

The simplex maximum raises an independent spectral question.
A product of coordinate integrals that uses every coordinate has a bounded
integral kernel.  An identity of ordered products therefore bounds the
essential spectrum by the maximum in one lower dimension.
Concentration on shrinking slices gives the matching lower bound.
A positive function concentrated near a face proves strict increase of
the maximum when a coordinate is added.  These constructions give a unique
positive maximizer in every finite dimension, without an arithmetic
distribution assumption.

\section{The finite product map}

For fixed integer shifts, which residue classes make every translate coprime
to a given modulus?  Coprimality to a product of coprime moduli is equivalent
to coprimality to each factor.  The Chinese remainder theorem then separates
the allowed classes into a product of finite sets.

Fix a nonempty finite set of distinct integers
$\mathcal H=\{h_1,\ldots,h_k\}$.  For a positive integer $q$, set
\[
 R_q=\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z,
 \qquad
 G_q(\mathcal H)
 =\bigl\{a\in R_q:a+h_i\in R_q^\times\text{ for }1\leq i\leq k\bigr\}.
\]
If $q_1$ and $q_2$ are coprime, the Chinese-remainder ring isomorphism is
\[
 c_{q_1,q_2}:R_{q_1q_2}\longrightarrow R_{q_1}\times R_{q_2},
 \qquad
 [a]_{q_1q_2}\longmapsto([a]_{q_1},[a]_{q_2}).
\]

\begin{proposition}[Coprime configuration factorization]
For coprime positive integers $q_1,q_2$, restriction of $c_{q_1,q_2}$ gives
the bijection
\[
 \gamma_{q_1,q_2}:G_{q_1q_2}(\mathcal H)
 \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }
 G_{q_1}(\mathcal H)\times G_{q_2}(\mathcal H).
\]
For three pairwise coprime moduli, the two iterated product maps agree after
the canonical identification of the two parenthesizations.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
An element $a+h_i$ is a unit in $R_{q_1q_2}$ if and only if its two images
are units in $R_{q_1}$ and $R_{q_2}$.  The ring isomorphism therefore carries
the defining conditions for $G_{q_1q_2}(\mathcal H)$ precisely to the defining
conditions for the displayed product.  The associativity statement follows
because either iterated map sends $[a]_{q_1q_2q_3}$ to
$([a]_{q_1},[a]_{q_2},[a]_{q_3})$.
\end{proof}

For a prime $p$, define
\[
 \Omega_p(\mathcal H)=\{-h_i\bmod p:1\leq i\leq k\}\subset\mathbb F_p,
 \qquad \nu_p(\mathcal H)=|\Omega_p(\mathcal H)|.
\]
Then $G_p(\mathcal H)=\mathbb F_p\setminus\Omega_p(\mathcal H)$.  Iterating
$\gamma$ gives, for every positive squarefree $q$, a specified bijection
\[
 G_q(\mathcal H)\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }
 \prod_{p\mid q}\bigl(\mathbb F_p\setminus\Omega_p(\mathcal H)\bigr),
 \qquad
 |G_q(\mathcal H)|=\prod_{p\mid q}(p-\nu_p(\mathcal H)).
\]

There is an equivalent statement for functions on residue classes.
For a finite set of primes $S$, let
$X_S=\prod_{p\in S}\mathbb F_p$ and give $X_S$ normalized counting
measure $\mu_S$.  Multiplication by
$q_S(a)=\prod_{p\in S}\mathbf1_{G_p(\mathcal H)}(a_p)$ is an
orthogonal projection $Q_S$ on $\ell^2(X_S,\mu_S;\mathbb R)$.
For disjoint $S,T$, the map
\[
 f\otimes g\longmapsto\bigl((a,b)\longmapsto f(a)g(b)\bigr)
\]
extends from the Hilbert tensor product of the two function spaces to
an isometric isomorphism onto $\ell^2(X_{S\cup T},\mu_{S\cup T})$.
Indeed, normalized sums factor, so inner products of elementary tensors
agree, and the residue indicator functions span both spaces.
This isomorphism carries $Q_S\otimes Q_T$ to $Q_{S\cup T}$.
The expectation $\omega_S(A)=\langle1,A1\rangle$ on linear endomorphisms
satisfies
\[
 \omega_S(Q_S)=\prod_{p\in S}(1-\nu_p(\mathcal H)/p),\qquad
 \omega_S(A^*A)=\|A1\|_2^2\geq0.
\]
The first identity counts the surviving residues; the second follows from
the definition of the adjoint.

\section{The infinite product}

For a prime $p$, the proportion of allowed residue classes is
$1-\nu_p(\mathcal H)/p$.  Each separate condition $a+h_i\not\equiv0\pmod p$
holds on a proportion $1-1/p$ of the classes.  Dividing the joint proportion
by the product of these separate proportions gives the following local
factor.  This normalization makes no assertion that the conditions are
independent.

\begin{definition}
The set $\mathcal H$ is \emph{admissible} if
$\nu_p(\mathcal H)<p$ for every prime $p$.  Its local factor is
\[
 \sigma_p(\mathcal H)
 =\frac{1-\nu_p(\mathcal H)/p}{(1-1/p)^k}.
\]
\end{definition}

Admissibility is exactly the positivity of every local factor.  It removes
all congruence obstructions, but it asserts no distribution theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Positive singular product]
If $\mathcal H$ is admissible, then
\[
 \mathfrak S(\mathcal H)=\prod_p\sigma_p(\mathcal H)
\]
converges to a finite, strictly positive real number.  More precisely,
\[
 \sigma_p(\mathcal H)=1-\frac{k(k-1)}{2p^2}+O_k(p^{-3})
\]
outside a finite set of primes.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put
\[
 \Delta_{\mathcal H}=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}|h_i-h_j|,
\]
with the empty product equal to $1$.  If $p\nmid\Delta_{\mathcal H}$, the
reductions of the $h_i$ are distinct, so $\nu_p(\mathcal H)=k$.  Thus, for all
but finitely many primes,
\[
 \sigma_p=(1-k/p)(1-1/p)^{-k}.
\]
For $p>k$, the convergent power series for the logarithm gives
\[
 \log\sigma_p
 =-\sum_{n\geq2}\frac{k^n-k}{n p^n}
 =-\frac{k(k-1)}{2p^2}+O_k(p^{-3}).
\]
Exponentiation gives the asserted expansion and, in particular,
$|\sigma_p-1|=O_k(p^{-2})$.  Hence
$\sum_p|\sigma_p-1|<\infty$.  Eventually $|\sigma_p-1|\leq1/2$, and then
$|\log\sigma_p|\leq2|\sigma_p-1|$.  The logarithmic series therefore
converges absolutely.  All its factors are positive by admissibility, so the
product is finite and strictly positive.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Effective product tail]\label{cor:product-tail}
Let $Y$ be an integer with $Y\geq2k$, and suppose every prime divisor of
$\Delta_{\mathcal H}$ is at most $Y$.  For admissible $\mathcal H$, put
$\mathfrak S_Y=\prod_{p\leq Y}\sigma_p(\mathcal H)$.  Then
\[
 e^{-k^2/Y}\mathfrak S_Y\leq\mathfrak S(\mathcal H)
 \leq\mathfrak S_Y.
\]
For $k=1$, $\mathfrak S_Y=\mathfrak S(\mathcal H)=1$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For $p>Y$, the logarithmic series in the preceding proof gives
\[
 0\leq-\log\sigma_p
 \leq\sum_{n\geq2}\frac{(k/p)^n}{2}
 =\frac{k^2}{2p^2(1-k/p)}\leq\frac{k^2}{p^2}.
\]
Sum over primes and use
$\sum_{n>Y}n^{-2}\leq\int_Y^\infty t^{-2}\,dt=Y^{-1}$.
Exponentiation proves both bounds.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The shifts $\{0,2\}$]
Let $\mathcal H=\{0,2\}$.  At the exceptional prime $2$, the two forbidden
classes coincide:
\[
 \Omega_2(\mathcal H)=\{0\},\qquad
 G_2(\mathcal H)=\{1\},\qquad
 \sigma_2=\frac{1-1/2}{(1-1/2)^2}=2.
\]
For every odd prime $p$, the forbidden classes $0$ and $-2$ are distinct, so
\[
 |G_p(\mathcal H)|=p-2,
 \qquad
 \sigma_p=\frac{1-2/p}{(1-1/p)^2}
 =\frac{p(p-2)}{(p-1)^2}
 =1-\frac{1}{(p-1)^2}.
\]
Consequently
\[
 \mathfrak S(\{0,2\})
 =2\prod_{p>2}\left(1-\frac{1}{(p-1)^2}\right)>0.
\]
The positivity and convergence of this constant are local statements.  The
assertion that infinitely many integers $n$ make both $n$ and $n+2$ prime is
not a consequence of them.
\end{example}

The cardinality $k$ alone does not determine the local factor.
At $p=3$, the pairs $\{0,2\}$ and $\{0,6\}$ have respectively two
and one forbidden residue classes.  Their factors are therefore
$(1-2/3)/(1-1/3)^2=3/4$ and $(1-1/3)/(1-1/3)^2=3/2$.

\section{A finite divisor-sum criterion}

To detect several prime translates at one integer, one can compare a
nonnegative weighted prime count with the total weight.  If the weighted
average exceeds an integer $m$, some integer with positive weight has more
than $m$ prime translates.  Squared divisor sums provide nonnegative weights
whose total weight can be computed from congruences.

Let $\mathcal H$ be admissible, and let $W$ be a positive squarefree integer
divisible by every prime factor of $\Delta_{\mathcal H}$.  The finite product
map gives
\[
 |G_W(\mathcal H)|=\prod_{p\mid W}(p-\nu_p(\mathcal H))>0.
\]
Choose an integer $v_0=v_0(W)$ whose class lies in $G_W(\mathcal H)$.  Thus
$\gcd(v_0+h_i,W)=1$ for every $i$.  Let $N$ be a positive integer such that
\[
 I_N=\{n\in\mathbb Z:N\leq n<2N,\ n\equiv v_0\pmod W\}
\]
is nonempty and $n+h_i\geq2$ for $n\in I_N$ and all $i$.  Let
$\mathcal D$ be a finite set of $k$-tuples of positive integers containing
$\boldsymbol 1=(1,\ldots,1)$.  Require
\[
 d_1\cdots d_k\text{ to be squarefree},
 \qquad \gcd(d_1\cdots d_k,W)=1
 \quad(\boldsymbol d\in\mathcal D).
\]
Define the divisor-incidence map by
\[
 T:\mathbb R^{\mathcal D}\longrightarrow\mathbb R^{I_N},
 \qquad
 (T\lambda)(n)=
 \sum_{\substack{\boldsymbol d\in\mathcal D\\
                  d_i\mid n+h_i\ (1\leq i\leq k)}}
 \lambda_{\boldsymbol d}
\]
and assign to $n\in I_N$ the weight
\[
 w_\lambda(n)=|(T\lambda)(n)|^2\geq0.
\]
These squared divisor sums are finite multidimensional Selberg weights
as in \cite[(2.4), (4.2)--(4.3)]{Maynard}.  To count prime translates, put
\[
 R_{\mathcal H}(n)=\sum_{i=1}^k\mathbf 1_{\mathbb P}(n+h_i),
 \qquad (M_Rf)(n)=R_{\mathcal H}(n)f(n),
\]
where $\mathbb P$ is the set of positive primes, and define
\[
 A=T^*T,\qquad B=T^*M_RT,
\]
with respect to the standard Euclidean inner products.

\begin{proposition}[Finite divisor-sum Rayleigh principle]\label{prop:finite}
For $\boldsymbol d,\boldsymbol e\in\mathcal D$, set
$q_i=\operatorname{lcm}(d_i,e_i)$.  The matrix entries are
\[
 A_{\boldsymbol d,\boldsymbol e}
 =\#\{n\in I_N:q_i\mid n+h_i\text{ for }1\leq i\leq k\}
\]
and, when the $q_i$ are pairwise coprime,
\[
 \left|A_{\boldsymbol d,\boldsymbol e}
       -\frac{N}{W\prod_{i=1}^kq_i}\right|\leq1.
\]
If they are not pairwise coprime, then
$A_{\boldsymbol d,\boldsymbol e}=0$.  Moreover,
\[
 B_{\boldsymbol d,\boldsymbol e}
 =\sum_{\substack{n\in I_N\\q_i\mid n+h_i\ (1\leq i\leq k)}}
 R_{\mathcal H}(n).
\]
For $\lambda\in\mathbb R^{\mathcal D}$, write
\[
 S_1(\lambda)=\|T\lambda\|_2^2,
 \qquad
 S_2(\lambda)=\langle T\lambda,M_RT\lambda\rangle.
\]
If $V=(\ker T)^\perp$ and $A_V,B_V$ denote the restrictions to $V$, then
$A_V$ is positive definite and
\[
 \max_{\lambda\notin\ker T}\frac{S_2(\lambda)}{S_1(\lambda)}
 =\lambda_{\max}\!\left(A_V^{-1/2}B_VA_V^{-1/2}\right)
 \leq \max_{n\in I_N}R_{\mathcal H}(n)\leq k.
\]
Consequently, if $m$ is a nonnegative integer and some $\lambda$ satisfies
\[
 S_1(\lambda)>0,
 \qquad S_2(\lambda)>mS_1(\lambda),
\]
then at least $m+1$ of $n+h_1,\ldots,n+h_k$ are prime for some $n\in I_N$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Every integer $W,q_1,\ldots,q_k$ is squarefree, and $\gcd(W,q_i)=1$.
If a prime $p$ divides both $q_i$ and
$q_j$ with $i\ne j$, simultaneous divisibility would give
$h_i\equiv h_j\pmod p$.  But then $p\mid\Delta_{\mathcal H}$, hence $p\mid W$,
contrary to $\gcd(W,q_iq_j)=1$.  Thus the congruences counted by
$A_{\boldsymbol d,\boldsymbol e}$ are inconsistent unless the $q_i$ are
pairwise coprime.  In the remaining case, the Chinese remainder theorem gives
one residue class modulo $W\prod_iq_i$.  Its intersection with an interval of
$N$ consecutive integers has cardinality differing from
$N/(W\prod_iq_i)$ by at most $1$.  Expanding the two inner products gives the
displayed entries of $A$ and $B$.

Since $\boldsymbol 1\in\mathcal D$ and $I_N$ is nonempty, $T$ is nonzero.  If
$0\ne x\in V$, then
\[
 \langle x,A_Vx\rangle=\|Tx\|_2^2>0,
\]
so $A_V$ is positive definite.  Both $A$ and $B$ annihilate $\ker T$ and
preserve $V$.  Orthogonal projection onto $V$, followed by the substitution
$y=A_V^{1/2}x$, therefore reduces the generalized quotient to
\[
 \frac{\langle x,B_Vx\rangle}{\langle x,A_Vx\rangle}
 =\frac{\langle y,A_V^{-1/2}B_VA_V^{-1/2}y\rangle}
        {\langle y,y\rangle}.
\]
Rayleigh--Ritz gives the equality with the largest eigenvalue.  Finally,
\[
 S_2(\lambda)
 =\sum_{n\in I_N}R_{\mathcal H}(n)|(T\lambda)(n)|^2
 \leq\left(\max_{n\in I_N}R_{\mathcal H}(n)\right)S_1(\lambda).
\]
If $S_2(\lambda)>mS_1(\lambda)$, some term with
$(T\lambda)(n)\ne0$ has $R_{\mathcal H}(n)>m$.  This integer-valued prime
count is therefore at least $m+1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Two divisor coefficients]
Take $\mathcal H=\{0,2\}$, $W=2$, $v_0=1$, $N=5$, and
$\mathcal D=\{(1,1),(3,1)\}$.  Then $I_N=\{5,7,9\}$.
The first divisor tuple contributes at every sample.  The second contributes
only at $n=9$, since $3\mid9$ but $3\nmid5,7$.  With rows ordered as
$5,7,9$ and columns as $(1,1),(3,1)$, this gives
\[
 T=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 (R_{\mathcal H}(5),R_{\mathcal H}(7),R_{\mathcal H}(9))=(2,1,1).
\]
Indeed, the pairs of translates are $(5,7)$, $(7,9)$, and $(9,11)$.
Thus
\[
 A=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 B=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
For $\lambda=(a,b)$, the weights are $a^2,a^2,(a+b)^2$, so
\[
 \frac{S_2(\lambda)}{S_1(\lambda)}
 =\frac{3a^2+(a+b)^2}{2a^2+(a+b)^2}
 \leq\frac32\qquad(\lambda\ne0).
\]
Equality holds at $(a,b)=(1,-1)$, where $S_1=2$ and $S_2=3$.
The strict threshold with $m=1$ therefore detects a pair of prime translates
in this interval, realized at $n=5$.  The maximum $3/2$ is smaller than the
largest sampled prime count, which is $2$.  Every permitted weight gives
equal mass to $5$ and $7$, so these weights cannot isolate the sample $5$.
This finite calculation supplies no asymptotic prime-count estimate.
\end{example}

Admissibility supplies the residue class $v_0\pmod W$, but it supplies no strict inequality
between $S_2$ and $mS_1$.  Neither positivity of
$\mathfrak S(\mathcal H)$ nor the finite identities above implies the threshold
inequality.  The comparison with an integral operator requires the additional
arithmetic estimates and the specified coefficient map below.

\section{The simplex operator}

Integrating one variable of a function produces the quadratic expressions
that occur in the limiting prime sum.  For two variables, the domain is
the triangle $\Delta_2=\{(x,y):x,y\geq0,\ x+y\leq1\}$.  The constant
function has squared integral norm $1/2$.  Its two fibre integrals are
$1-y$ and $1-x$, so their squared norms sum to $2/3$.
The resulting Rayleigh value is $4/3$.

For a positive integer $k$, define
\[
 \Delta_k=\{t\in[0,\infty)^k:t_1+\cdots+t_k\leq1\},
 \qquad H_k=L^2(\Delta_k,dt;\mathbb R).
\]
All integrals use ordinary Lebesgue measure, without volume normalization.
Write $\ip fg=\int_{\Delta_k}fg$ and $\norm f^2=\ip ff$.
Functions are equivalence classes modulo equality almost everywhere.
No boundary conditions are imposed.  When $k=1$, the space $\Delta_0$
is a single point of measure one.  For $1\leq i\leq k$, write
$t_{\widehat i}$ for the coordinates other than $t_i$, and put
$\ell_i(s)=1-\sum_{j\ne i}s_j$ on $\Delta_{k-1}$.
Define the fibre integral
\[
 T_i:H_k\longrightarrow L^2(\Delta_{k-1}),\qquad
 (T_iF)(s)=\int_0^{\ell_i(s)}F(s_1,\ldots,u,\ldots,s_k)\,du.
\]
The notation $T_i$ denotes a continuum map.  The finite incidence map
of Proposition~\ref{prop:finite} will be denoted by $T_N$ below.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:simplex}
Each $T_i$ is bounded with norm one.  Its adjoint and its product with
the adjoint are
\[
 (T_i^*g)(t)=g(t_{\widehat i}),\qquad T_iT_i^*g=\ell_i g.
\]
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spine-operator}
 K_k=\sum_{i=1}^kT_i^*T_i,
 \qquad
 \langle F,K_kF\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^k\|T_iF\|_2^2
\end{equation}
defines a bounded positive self-adjoint operator on $H_k$, with
$0\leq K_k\leq kI$.  If
\[
 M_k=\sup_{0\ne F\in H_k}
       \frac{\sum_i\|T_iF\|_2^2}{\|F\|_2^2},
\]
then $M_k=\|K_k\|$ and $2k/(k+1)\leq M_k\leq k$.
The same supremum results from smooth functions with compact support
in the interior of $\Delta_k$.
For $k\geq2$, no nonzero function attains the norm of an individual $T_i$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fibrewise Cauchy--Schwarz gives
\[
 |T_iF(s)|^2\leq\ell_i(s)
       \int_0^{\ell_i(s)}|F(s_1,\ldots,u,\ldots,s_k)|^2\,du.
\]
Since $0\leq\ell_i\leq1$, integration proves boundedness.
For $k\geq2$, $\ell_i(t_{\widehat i})<1$ almost everywhere in
$\Delta_k$, so the integrated bound is strict for every nonzero $F$.
Fubini proves the adjoint formula.  Hence $T_iT_i^*$ is multiplication
by $\ell_i$, whose essential supremum is one.  This proves
$\|T_i\|=1$ and all operator assertions.
The Rayleigh characterization of a bounded positive self-adjoint operator
gives $M_k=\|K_k\|$.

For $F=1$, repeated integration gives
\[
 \|1\|_2^2=\frac1{k!},\qquad
 \|T_i1\|_2^2=\int_{\Delta_{k-1}}(1-\textstyle\sum s_j)^2\,ds
                  =\frac2{(k+1)!}.
\]
These identities follow by integrating the last variable and induction
on the dimension.  They prove the lower bound.
Smooth functions with compact support in the interior are dense in $H_k$:
first restrict a function to compact subsets of the interior, then mollify
its zero extension.  Boundedness of $K_k$ makes the numerator continuous
in $L^2$.  Approximation therefore preserves the supremum.
\end{proof}

We call $\|F\|_2^2$ the mass of $F$ and $\langle F,K_kF\rangle$ its energy.

This $K_k$ is the operator in \cite[(7.1)--(7.2), p.~405]{Maynard}.
Maynard also excludes test functions with a zero fibre norm.
That restriction leaves the supremum unchanged.  Choose one nonnegative
smooth interior function $\psi\ne0$, for which every $T_i\psi\ne0$.
For any fixed $F$, the equation $T_i(F+a\psi)=0$ excludes at most one
real number $a$.  A sequence $a\to0$ avoiding these finitely many values
proves the assertion.  Real and complex $L^2$ give the same supremum,
because numerator and denominator split over real and imaginary parts.

\begin{example}[Two polynomial test functions]\label{ex:triangle-ritz}
On $\Delta_2$, take $F(x,y)=a+b(x+y)$ with real coefficients $a,b$.
The mass and fibre forms have matrices
\[
 A_0=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&1/3\\1/3&1/4\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 B_0=\begin{pmatrix}2/3&5/12\\5/12&4/15\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Indeed, integration of monomials gives
$\int_{\Delta_2}x^ry^s\,dx\,dy=r!s!/(r+s+2)!$ for nonnegative
integers $r,s$.  The two fibre polynomials for the basis $1,x+y$
are $1-y$ and $(1-y^2)/2$ in the first direction.
The second direction gives the same integrals.
These formulas determine every matrix entry.
Since $\det A_0=1/72>0$, the maximal quotient is the larger root of
\[
          720\det(B_0-zA_0)=10z^2-16z+3=0.
\]
Thus $M_2\geq(8+\sqrt{34})/10>4/3$.
These polynomials belong to $H_2$.  Approximation gives the same supremum
over smooth functions with compact support in the interior.
\end{example}

\subsection{Compactness and approximate eigenvectors}

A bounded sequence can lose its mass from every fixed test function.
We write $f_n\rightharpoonup0$, and say it converges weakly to zero,
when $\ip g{f_n}\to0$ for every $g$ in the Hilbert space.
A bounded operator $C$ is \emph{compact} if every bounded sequence has a
subsequence whose images under $C$ converge in norm.
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:compact-weak}
 f_n\rightharpoonup0,\quad \sup_n\norm{f_n}<\infty
                    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\norm{Cf_n}\to0.
\end{equation}
Otherwise a subsequence with image norms bounded below would have a
nonzero norm limit.  Its weak limit is zero because
$\ip g{Cf_n}=\ip{C^*g}{f_n}\to0$, a contradiction.

We will use weak subsequences on separable Hilbert spaces.
Here is the required construction.  Choose an orthonormal basis $e_j$,
and successively extract subsequences on which $\ip{e_j}{f_n}$ converges.
The diagonal subsequence has limiting coordinates with bounded sum of
squares, by applying the norm bound to every finite sum.
Those coordinates define a Hilbert-space vector $f$.
Approximation of a test vector by its first finitely many coordinates
proves $f_n\rightharpoonup f$ along this subsequence.
The spaces $H_d$ are separable: simple functions on boxes with rational
endpoints form a countable dense family, after rationalizing their values.

\begin{lemma}[Bounded integral kernels]\label{lem:kernel}
Let $D\subset\mathbb R^d$ be measurable and have finite measure.
If $h\in L^2(D\times D)$, then
\[
                  (C_hf)(x)=\int_D h(x,y)f(y)\,dy
\]
is compact and $\norm{C_h}\leq\norm h_{L^2(D\times D)}$.
In particular, a measurable kernel bounded by one on
$\D_3\times\D_3$ gives $\norm{C_h}\leq1/6$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Cauchy--Schwarz in $y$ followed by integration in $x$ gives the norm bound.
Finite sums $\sum_j a_j(x)b_j(y)$ are dense in $L^2(D\times D)$.
This follows by approximating measurable functions with simple functions
on rectangles.  Their integral operators have finite-dimensional range.
The norm bound makes $C_h$ an operator norm limit of those operators.
Such a limit is compact: approximate the image of the unit ball by a
finite-dimensional bounded set within any prescribed positive error.
Each such set has a finite net; these nets make every image sequence
have a Cauchy subsequence.  The last bound uses $|\D_3|=1/6$.
\end{proof}

The spectrum of a bounded self-adjoint operator $B$ consists of the real
numbers $\lambda$ for which $B-\lambda I$ has no bounded inverse.
Working with the complexification gives the usual complex spectrum.
It has no nonreal points: for $\operatorname{Im}z\ne0$,
$\norm{(B-z)f}\geq|\operatorname{Im}z|\norm f$.
Its range is closed and dense, since the orthogonal complement of that
range is $\ker(B-\bar z)=0$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:essential}
The essential spectrum of $B$, denoted by $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(B)$,
is the set of real $\lambda$ admitting a sequence with
\[
 \norm{f_n}=1,\qquad f_n\rightharpoonup0,
                  \qquad\norm{(B-\lambda I)f_n}\to0.
\]
Such a sequence is called a singular sequence at $\lambda$.
Its existence rules out bounded invertibility.
\end{definition}

This definition distinguishes eigenvectors of finite multiplicity from
approximate eigenvectors that disappear weakly.
For example, multiplication by $x$ on $L^2(0,1)$ has a singular sequence
at every $\lambda\in[0,1]$.
Use normalized indicators of intervals shrinking to $\lambda$.
The residual is at most the interval diameter, and weak convergence
follows from absolute continuity of the integral of $|g|^2$.

\begin{lemma}[The discrete spectrum above an essential bound]
\label{lem:spectral}
For a bounded self-adjoint $B$ on a separable Hilbert space,
a real number $\lambda$ is outside
$\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(B)$ precisely when $\ker(B-\lambda I)$ is finite
dimensional and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bounded-below}
 \norm{(B-\lambda I)f}\geq c\norm f
       \quad\text{on }\ker(B-\lambda I)^\perp
\end{equation}
for some $c>0$.
If such a $\lambda$ is in the spectrum, it is an isolated eigenvalue
of finite multiplicity.
For a nonzero bounded positive $B$, the number
\[
                   b=\sup_{\norm f=1}\ip f{Bf}
\]
equals $\norm B$ and belongs to its spectrum.
If $b$ is larger than every essential spectral value, it is attained.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
An infinite-dimensional kernel contains an orthonormal sequence, which
is weakly zero and is a singular sequence.
If \eqref{eq:bounded-below} fails, choose unit vectors in the kernel
complement with residuals tending to zero.
A weakly convergent subsequence has limit in both the kernel and its
orthogonal complement, hence has weak limit zero.
Conversely, a singular sequence has projections onto any fixed
finite-dimensional kernel tending to zero.
Inequality~\eqref{eq:bounded-below} then contradicts its unit norm.

Let $E=\ker(B-\lambda I)$ and assume the stated finite kernel and bound.
Self-adjointness makes $E^\perp$ invariant.
On $E^\perp$, the range of $B-\lambda I$ is closed by the bound and
dense because its orthogonal complement is its kernel.
It is therefore all of $E^\perp$, with inverse norm at most $c^{-1}$.
If $E=0$, $\lambda$ is outside the spectrum.
Otherwise, for $0<|t-\lambda|<c$, the inverse on $E^\perp$ follows
from the convergent geometric series for the bounded perturbation
$(\lambda-t)I$.  On $E$, the operator is multiplication by $\lambda-t$.
Thus $\lambda$ is isolated and has finite multiplicity.

For positive $B$, its quadratic form satisfies
$|\ip f{Bg}|^2\leq\ip f{Bf}\ip g{Bg}$.
To see this, expand the nonnegative quadratic polynomial
$\ip{f+tg}{B(f+tg)}$ and use its discriminant.
It follows that $\norm B\leq b$; the reverse inequality is immediate.
Moreover, $\norm{Bf}^2\leq b\ip f{Bf}$, by applying this form inequality
to $f$ and unit test vectors.
Choose unit $f_n$ with $\ip{f_n}{Bf_n}\to b$.
Then
\[
 \norm{(B-bI)f_n}^2\leq b\bigl(b-\ip{f_n}{Bf_n}\bigr)\to0.
\]
Thus $b$ belongs to the spectrum.
If it lies above the essential spectrum, the preceding argument supplies
an eigenvector, which attains the quotient.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Distance from compact operators]\label{prop:noncompact}
For $k\geq2$ and every compact operator $C:H_k\to H_k$,
\[
                         \|K_k-C\|\geq1.
\]
In particular, no sequence of finite-rank operators converges to $K_k$
in operator norm.  For $k=1$, $K_1F=(\int_0^1F)1$ has rank one.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fix $0<\varepsilon<1$ and one coordinate $i$.
The set $E=\{s\in\Delta_{k-1}:\ell_i(s)>1-\varepsilon\}$
has positive measure.  Split $E$ into infinitely many disjoint measurable
sets of positive measure, and let $g_j$ be their normalized indicators.
Define
\[
 F_j(t)=\frac{g_j(t_{\widehat i})}
                 {\sqrt{\ell_i(t_{\widehat i})}}.
\]
Fubini shows that the $F_j$ are orthonormal in $H_k$ and
\[
 \langle F_j,K_kF_j\rangle
 \geq\|T_iF_j\|_2^2
 =\int_E\ell_i(s)|g_j(s)|^2\,ds>1-\varepsilon.
\]
An orthonormal sequence converges weakly to zero.  Compactness gives
$\|CF_j\|_2\to0$: otherwise compactness produces a nonzero norm limit
along a subsequence, contradicting weak convergence.
Thus $\|K_k-C\|\geq1-\varepsilon$.  Let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{An attained maximum in two dimensions}

Noncompactness alone does not determine whether a Rayleigh maximum is
attained.  The following computation makes this distinction explicit.
It is the two-dimensional calculation in
\cite[Corollaries 35--36]{Polymath}.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:m2}
Let $w>0$ be the unique solution of $we^w=e^{-1}$, and set
$\lambda=(1-w)^{-1}$.  Then $M_2=\lambda<3/2$.
There is a continuous strictly positive function $\psi$ on $\Delta_2$
with $K_2\psi=M_2\psi$ and $\|\psi\|_2=1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The function $u\mapsto ue^u$ increases strictly from zero to infinity
on $[0,\infty)$, so $w$ exists uniquely.
Its equation implies $0<w<1$ and
\[
 \log\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-1}=2-\frac1\lambda.
\]
For $0\leq x\leq1$, define
\[
 f(x)=\frac1{\lambda-1+x}
     +\frac1{2\lambda-1}\log\frac{\lambda-x}{\lambda-1+x}.
\]
The logarithmic identity gives $f(1)=0$.
Writing $a=\lambda-1+x$, $b=\lambda-x$, and $c=2\lambda-1=a+b$,
one obtains
\[
 f'(x)=-\frac1{a^2}-\frac1c\left(\frac1b+\frac1a\right)<0,
 \qquad
 \frac{d}{dx}(af(x))=\frac1c\log\frac ba-\frac1b=-f(1-x).
\]
Both $af(x)$ and $\int_0^{1-x}f(u)\,du$ vanish at $x=1$.
Their derivatives agree, so
\[
                 \int_0^{1-x}f(u)\,du=(\lambda-1+x)f(x).
\]
The continuous function $F(x,y)=f(x)+f(y)$ is strictly positive on
$\Delta_2$.  Indeed, $f(x)>0$ for $x<1$, and the two coordinates cannot
both equal one.  The preceding identity gives the
fibre equation
\[
 \int_0^{1-y}F(u,y)\,du+\int_0^{1-x}F(x,u)\,du
                       =\lambda F(x,y).
\]
Thus $K_2F=\lambda F$ and $M_2\geq\lambda$.

For any $u\in H_2$, weighted Cauchy--Schwarz on each fibre gives
\[
 |T_i u(s)|^2\leq (T_iF)(s)
                 \int_0^{\ell_i(s)}\frac{|u(s_1,\ldots,t,\ldots)|^2}
                                              {F(s_1,\ldots,t,\ldots)}\,dt.
\]
The function $F$ has a positive minimum on the compact triangle.
Integrate and sum over $i$ to obtain
\[
 \langle u,K_2u\rangle
 \leq\int_{\Delta_2}|u|^2\frac{K_2F}{F}\,dt
 =\lambda\|u\|_2^2.
\]
This proves the reverse bound and attainment.  Put $\psi=F/\|F\|_2$.
Finally, $e^{4/3}>1+4/3+(4/3)^2/2=29/9>3$.
Thus $(1/3)e^{1/3}>e^{-1}$, which gives $w<1/3$ and $M_2<3/2$.
\end{proof}

For a bounded positive operator $B$, equality in its maximal Rayleigh
quotient is equivalent to $BF=\|B\|F$ for a nonzero $F$.
The estimate
$\|(B-\|B\|I)F\|^2\leq\|B\|(\|B\|\|F\|^2-\langle F,BF\rangle)$
from Lemma~\ref{lem:spectral} proves the assertion.
The three-dimensional maximum follows from the next spectral argument.

\section{The three-variable spectral problem}\label{sec:three-variable}

Integration in one coordinate retains arbitrary dependence on the other
two coordinates.  This permits concentrating sequences even though a
maximal eigenfunction may exist.
We now determine both the essential spectrum and the maximal eigenspace
on the tetrahedron $\Delta_3$.
Write
\[
 A_i=T_i^*T_i\quad(1\leq i\leq3),\qquad K=K_3=A_1+A_2+A_3,
 \qquad \kappa=M_2.
\]
Every $A_i$ preserves nonnegative functions, since it integrates on a fibre.
This property differs from positivity of the quadratic form,
although $A_i$ has both properties.
Let $\psi$ be the positive unit triangle eigenfunction of
Proposition~\ref{prop:m2}.  We keep $K_2$ for the operator on $H_2$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:simplex} gives $M_3\geq3/2$ from the constant
function.  A linear polynomial gives a stronger exact bound.

\begin{example}[A linear test function]\label{ex:linear-test}
Let $s=x+y+z$ and $f=a+bs$.
The mass and fibre quadratic forms on the basis $1,s$ have matrices
\[
 G=\begin{pmatrix}1/6&1/8\\1/8&1/10\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 B=\begin{pmatrix}1/4&7/40\\7/40&1/8\end{pmatrix}.
\]
To compute $G$, use
$\int_{\D_3}h(x+y+z)=\tfrac12\int_0^1s^2h(s)\,ds$.
For $B$, write $r$ for the sum of the two remaining coordinates.
The fibre integrals of $1,s$ are $1-r,(1-r^2)/2$, and
$\int_{\D_2}h(x+y)=\int_0^1r h(r)\,dr$.
These one-variable integrals give all the displayed entries.
For $f_0=1-3s/4$, they give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:linear-rayleigh}
 \norm{f_0}_2^2=\frac{17}{480},\qquad
 \ip{f_0}{Kf_0}=\frac{37}{640},\qquad
 \frac{\ip{f_0}{Kf_0}}{\norm{f_0}_2^2}=\frac{111}{68}.
\end{equation}
The maximum on this two-dimensional space is also explicit:
\[
 960\det(B-uG)=u^2-2u+\frac35,
 \qquad M_3\geq1+\sqrt{\frac25}.
\]
These are finite-dimensional lower bounds.  Attainment in $H_3$ requires
control of sequences outside this test space.
\end{example}

\subsection{Two-coordinate slices}

Fixing the third coordinate leaves a scaled copy of $\Delta_2$.
The change of variables must account for both the slice measure and the
length of each integrating fibre.

Let $S_{12}=A_1+A_2$, and write $a=1-z$ for $0<z<1$.
The change of variables
\begin{equation}\label{eq:slice-unitary}
 (\mathcal Uf)(u,v,z)=a f(au,av,z)
\end{equation}
is an isometric isomorphism from $H_3$ onto
$L^2(\D_2\times(0,1),du\,dv\,dz)$.
Indeed, $dx\,dy=a^2du\,dv$, and the inverse is
$f(x,y,z)=a^{-1}(\mathcal Uf)(x/a,y/a,z)$.
Endpoints in $z$ have measure zero.
Changing one integration variable gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pair-decomposition}
           (\mathcal U S_{12}\mathcal U^{-1}g)(u,v,z)
                         =(1-z)(K_2g(\cdot,\cdot,z))(u,v).
\end{equation}
Consequently $\norm{S_{12}}\leq\kappa$.
Functions of the form $g(u,v,z)=\psi(u,v)q(z)$ with $q$ concentrated
near zero give the reverse inequality.
Coordinate permutations give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pair-norm}
                 \norm{A_1+A_2}=\norm{A_1+A_3}
                       =\norm{A_2+A_3}=\kappa.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Products that integrate every coordinate}

The pair sums retain one coordinate.
A product can remove this remaining dependence by integrating that
coordinate as well.  The order matters, so we keep every intermediate point.
For example, direct iteration gives
\[
 (A_1A_2A_3f)(x)
 =\int_{\D_3}
    \mathbf1_{y_1+x_2+x_3\leq1}
    \mathbf1_{y_1+y_2+x_3\leq1}f(y)\,dy.
\]
This kernel is bounded by one, so the product is compact.
The individual $A_i$ and the pair sums need not be compact.

\begin{lemma}[A kernel for each complete word]\label{lem:words}
Let $i_1,\ldots,i_n\in\{1,2,3\}$, and suppose each of $1,2,3$
occurs.  Then $A_{i_1}\cdots A_{i_n}$ has a measurable integral kernel
on $\D_3\times\D_3$ with values in $[0,1]$.
It is compact, with operator norm at most $1/6$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Extend functions by zero outside $\D_3$ to the unit cube.
Starting at $x^{(0)}=x$, let $x^{(r)}$ be obtained from $x^{(r-1)}$
by replacing coordinate $i_r$ with $t_r\in[0,1]$.
Successive integration gives, initially for bounded $f$,
\[
 (A_{i_1}\cdots A_{i_n}f)(x)
 =\int_{[0,1]^n}
      \left(\prod_{r=1}^n\mathbf1_{\D_3}(x^{(r)})\right)
                          f(x^{(n)})\,dt_1\cdots dt_n.
\]
For each coordinate $j$, let $r_j$ be its last occurrence in the word.
The final coordinate is $y_j=t_{r_j}$.
These are three distinct integration variables.
Rename the other $n-3$ variables as $v$ and apply Fubini.
The resulting kernel is
\[
 h(x,y)=\int_{[0,1]^{n-3}}
                \prod_{r=1}^n\mathbf1_{\D_3}(x^{(r)}(x,y,v))\,dv.
\]
It is measurable and lies between zero and one.
For $n=3$ the integral has no remaining variables and means the product
of the indicators.  Lemma~\ref{lem:kernel} proves compactness and the norm
bound.  Density of bounded functions extends the identity to $H_3$.
\end{proof}

Expand powers as sums of ordered words, without commuting the factors.
For every integer $n\geq1$, inclusion--exclusion gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:word-identity}
 \begin{split}
 K^n={}&(A_1+A_2)^n+(A_1+A_3)^n+(A_2+A_3)^n\\
      &-A_1^n-A_2^n-A_3^n+C_n,
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
where $C_n$ is the sum of words containing all three indices.
A one-index word occurs in two pair sums and one subtracted term.
A two-index word occurs in exactly one pair sum.
Only the three-index words remain for $C_n$.
Thus $C_n$ is compact, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:remainder-bound}
          \norm{K^n-C_n}\leq3\kappa^n+3.
\end{equation}
Here $\norm{A_i}\leq1$ follows from Proposition~\ref{prop:simplex}.
There are $3^n-3\cdot2^n+3$ complete words.
The proof even supplies a kernel bound
$0\leq h_{C_n}\leq3^n-3\cdot2^n+3$.
The sum $C_n$ is self-adjoint by \eqref{eq:word-identity}.
An individual word need not be self-adjoint; its nonnegative kernel
alone does not assert positivity of its quadratic form.

\subsection{The essential interval}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:essential}
For the three-dimensional simplex operator,
\[
                         \sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(K)=[0,\kappa].
\]
In particular, the upper essential spectral threshold is $M_2=\kappa$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
First let $\lambda$ admit a singular sequence $f_j$.
Positivity and $K\leq3I$ give $0\leq\lambda\leq3$.
For every fixed integer $n$, the identity
\[
 K^n-\lambda^n I=(K-\lambda I)
           \sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\lambda^rK^{n-1-r}
\]
shows that $\norm{(K^n-\lambda^n I)f_j}\to0$.
Compactness gives $C_nf_j\to0$ in norm.
Taking the limit in \eqref{eq:remainder-bound} gives
$\lambda^n\leq3\kappa^n+3$.
Since $\kappa>1$, taking $n$th roots and then $n\to\infty$ gives
$\lambda\leq\kappa$.

Conversely, fix $z_0\in[0,1]$ and $\lambda=(1-z_0)\kappa$.
Choose intervals $E_j\subset(0,1)$ with positive lengths $\varepsilon_j\to0$
and $\delta_j=\sup_{z\in E_j}|z-z_0|\to0$.
Let $q_j=\varepsilon_j^{-1/2}\mathbf1_{E_j}$ and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:singular-sequence}
 f_j(x,y,z)=\frac{q_j(z)}{1-z}
                \psi\left(\frac{x}{1-z},\frac{y}{1-z}\right).
\end{equation}
The isometry \eqref{eq:slice-unitary} gives $\norm{f_j}_2=1$.
For every $g\in H_3$,
\[
 |\ip g{f_j}|\leq\norm{\mathbf1_{z\in E_j}g}_2\longrightarrow0,
\]
by absolute continuity of the integral.
Equation~\eqref{eq:pair-decomposition} gives
$\norm{(S_{12}-\lambda I)f_j}\leq\kappa\delta_j$.
The third fibre integral obeys a different estimate.
Its integrand is supported in $E_j$, so Cauchy--Schwarz gives
\[
 \norm{T_3f_j}_2^2
 \leq\varepsilon_j\norm{f_j}_2^2=\varepsilon_j,
 \qquad\norm{A_3f_j}_2\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon_j}.
\]
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:singular-error}
              \norm{(K-\lambda I)f_j}_2
                     \leq\kappa\delta_j+\sqrt{\varepsilon_j}\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
This includes $z_0=0$, where mass approaches a triangular face,
and $z_0=1$, where the triangular sections shrink to a vertex.
Thus every $\lambda\in[0,\kappa]$ belongs to the essential spectrum.
\end{proof}

The upper endpoint is not merely a lower bound from one family of
concentrating functions.  Identity~\eqref{eq:word-identity} excludes every
larger value, including other possible patterns of concentration or oscillation.
The singular sequence at $\kappa$ also proves
\[
                        \norm{K-C}\geq\kappa
                \quad\text{for every compact }C:H_3\to H_3.
\]
Apply $K-C$ to that sequence and use \eqref{eq:compact-weak}.

\subsection{Attainment, uniqueness, and a convergent construction}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:attainment}
The number $M_3=\norm K$ is an isolated eigenvalue of finite multiplicity,
and
\[
              M_3\geq1+\sqrt{2/5}\geq111/68>3/2>\kappa.
\]
Its eigenspace is one-dimensional.  There is a unique unit eigenfunction
$\Phi$ that is positive almost everywhere.
It is invariant under all coordinate permutations.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The lower bounds follow from Example~\ref{ex:linear-test}, and the upper
essential threshold follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:essential}.
Lemma~\ref{lem:spectral} gives the isolated eigenvalue and finite multiplicity.
Take a real eigenfunction $f\ne0$ at $M_3$.
Replacing $f$ by $|f|$ does not decrease any squared fibre integral,
and preserves its norm.  Maximality therefore makes $|f|$ another
maximizer.  The final estimate in Lemma~\ref{lem:spectral}, applied
with $b=M_3$, shows that it is an eigenfunction.
This gives a nonnegative unit eigenfunction $\Phi$.

To prove strict positivity, consider
$L=A_1A_2A_3A_1A_2A_3$.
In its kernel formula, the path from $x$ to $y$ first replaces all
coordinates by $v=(v_1,v_2,v_3)$ and then replaces them by $y$.
For interior points $x,y\in\D_3$, every sufficiently small positive
$v_i$ makes all six intermediate points lie in $\D_3$:
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 (v_1,x_2,x_3),\ (v_1,v_2,x_3),\ (v_1,v_2,v_3),\\
 (y_1,v_2,v_3),\ (y_1,y_2,v_3),\ (y_1,y_2,y_3).
 \end{gathered}
\]
For example, it suffices to take
\[
 0<v_i<\tfrac14\min\{1-x_2-x_3,\ 1-x_3,\ 1-y_1,
                                      \ 1-y_1-y_2,\ 1\}.
\]
The minimum is positive for interior $x,y$.
Hence the kernel of $L$ is strictly positive there.
Every coordinate integral preserves nonnegative functions.
Since $K^6$ contains $L$ among its summands,
it takes each nonzero nonnegative function to a strictly positive
function almost everywhere.  The identity
$\Phi=M_3^{-6}K^6\Phi$ proves strict positivity.

For simplicity, suppose a real top eigenfunction $g$ changes sign.
Write $g=g_+-g_-$ with $g_+,g_-\geq0$ and disjoint supports.
Both parts are nonzero, and the positive kernel of $L$ gives
$\ip{g_+}{K^6g_-}>0$.
Self-adjointness gives
\[
 \ip{|g|}{K^6|g|}-\ip g{K^6g}=4\ip{g_+}{K^6g_-}>0.
\]
But $\norm{K^6}\leq M_3^6$ and
$\ip g{K^6g}=M_3^6\norm g^2$, a contradiction.
Thus every real top eigenfunction has one sign.
Two independent ones would give a nonzero top eigenfunction orthogonal
to $\Phi$, which must change sign because $\Phi>0$ almost everywhere.
This proves simplicity.

For a permutation $\sigma$, the map
$(U_\sigma f)(t)=f(t_{\sigma(1)},t_{\sigma(2)},t_{\sigma(3)})$
is an isometry, and direct substitution gives
$U_\sigma U_\tau=U_{\sigma\tau}$.
A change of integration variable shows that it permutes
the three $A_i$, so $U_\sigma K=KU_\sigma$.
The function $U_\sigma\Phi$ is again a positive unit top eigenfunction.
Simplicity forces $U_\sigma\Phi=\Phi$.
\end{proof}

Existence need not stop at an unnamed eigenvector.
The next result constructs it through a sequence whose terms are explicit
polynomials with rational coefficients.

\begin{theorem}[Polynomial construction of the maximizer]\label{thm:power}
Set $p_0=1$ and $p_{n+1}=Kp_n$.  Then
\[
                   \frac{p_n}{\norm{p_n}_2}\longrightarrow\Phi
                         \quad\text{in }H_3.
\]
Every $p_n$ is a symmetric polynomial with rational coefficients.
Its Rayleigh quotient converges to $M_3$.
More precisely, there are constants $c>0$, $0\leq r<M_3$, and
$h\perp\Phi$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:power-error}
 1=c\Phi+h,
 \qquad\norm{M_3^{-n}p_n-c\Phi}_2
               \leq(r/M_3)^n\norm h_2.
\end{equation}
No numerical value of $r$ is asserted.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The closed subspace $\Phi^\perp$ is invariant under $K$.
Its restricted operator is positive and has norm $r\leq M_3$.
If $r=M_3$, the last part of Lemma~\ref{lem:spectral} supplies unit
vectors $g_n\perp\Phi$ with $(K-M_3)g_n\to0$.
A weak subsequential limit lies in the top eigenspace and in
$\Phi^\perp$, hence is zero.
This would be a singular sequence at $M_3>\kappa$.
Therefore $r<M_3$.

Set $c=\ip1\Phi>0$ and $h=1-c\Phi$.
The decomposition is orthogonal and
$M_3^{-n}p_n=c\Phi+M_3^{-n}K^nh$.
The norm of the second term is at most $(r/M_3)^n\norm h$.
Its decay proves \eqref{eq:power-error} and convergence after normalization.
Continuity of the quadratic form proves convergence of the Rayleigh values.

To see that the construction is explicit, for a monomial
$x^ay^bz^c$ one has
\[
 A_1(x^ay^bz^c)=\frac{y^bz^c(1-y-z)^{a+1}}{a+1},
\]
with analogous formulas for $A_2,A_3$.
These operations preserve rational polynomial coefficients, and their
sum preserves coordinate symmetry.
Starting from $1$ proves both polynomial assertions.
\end{proof}

The integral of every monomial in this construction is exact:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:monomial-integral}
 \int_{\D_3}x^ay^bz^c\,dx\,dy\,dz
                      =\frac{a!b!c!}{(a+b+c+3)!}.
\end{equation}
Successive integration reduces this to
$\int_0^1t^a(1-t)^b\,dt=a!b!/(a+b+1)!$.
For nonnegative integers, integration by parts proves the latter by
induction, starting with $b=0$.
Thus the squared polynomial norms and Rayleigh values require only rational arithmetic.
For example,
\[
 p_1=3-2(x+y+z),\qquad
 \frac{\ip{p_0}{Kp_0}}{\norm{p_0}_2^2}=\frac32,
 \qquad
 \frac{\ip{p_1}{Kp_1}}{\norm{p_1}_2^2}=\frac{13}{8}.
\]

Continuing the same rational operations gives
\[
 \frac{\ip{p_2}{Kp_2}}{\norm{p_2}_2^2}=\frac{1461}{892},\qquad
 M_3\geq\frac{\ip{p_8}{Kp_8}}{\norm{p_8}_2^2}
       =\frac{215586362164143303}{130964453019307421}.
\]
The recurrence defines every coefficient of these polynomials, and
\eqref{eq:monomial-integral} evaluates their mass and energy.
These fractions are lower bounds; the convergence theorem determines their
limiting value without giving a closed expression for $M_3$.

The essential interval describes loss of compactness through shrinking
coordinate strips.  The largest eigenvalue lies strictly above that interval,
so this loss cannot occur for a maximizing sequence orthogonal to its
eigenfunction.  The resulting gap proves both variational attainment and
convergence of the polynomial construction.
An exact closed expression for $M_3$, a numerical value for the gap,
and boundary regularity of $\Phi$ are separate questions.
The following dimension comparison extends attainment to every finite $k$.


\section{Adding coordinates to the simplex}\label{all:section}

We now treat every finite dimension on the same Lebesgue simplex spaces.
To display the dimension in the notation, write $T_{k,i}$ for the map $T_i$
acting on $H_k$, and set $A_{k,i}=T_{k,i}^*T_{k,i}$.
Thus $K_k=\sum_iA_{k,i}$ is exactly the operator already defined in
Proposition~\ref{prop:simplex}.
Its quadratic quotient has supremum $M_k=\|K_k\|\leq k$.
A product that integrates every coordinate will be compact, while a
function supported near a face will prove $M_{k+1}>M_k$.

Here and below $n!=1\cdot2\cdots n$ for positive integers $n$, with
$0!=1$.  Successive integration gives, for integers $b\geq0$,
\begin{equation}\label{all:eq:slack}
 \int_{\Delta_k}(1-t_1-\cdots-t_k)^b\,dt=\frac{b!}{(k+b)!}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, integration in the last variable divides by $b+1$ and increases
the exponent by one.  Repeating this $k$ times proves the formula.
In particular $|\Delta_k|=1/k!$.
For $f=1$, the numerator in \eqref{eq:spine-operator} is $2k/(k+1)!$,
so $M_k\geq2k/(k+1)>0$.
For $f(t)=1-\sum_i t_i$, each line integral equals $\ell_i^2/2$.
Formula~\eqref{all:eq:slack} gives
\begin{equation}\label{all:eq:linear-bound}
 \|f\|^2=\frac2{(k+2)!},\qquad
 \langle f,K_kf\rangle=\frac{6k}{(k+3)!},\qquad
 M_k\geq\frac{3k}{k+3}.
\end{equation}
These elementary calculations will also distinguish two ways of
concentrating a function near a face.

\subsection{Products of integrals}

A sequence of coordinate integrations can remove the dependence left by
any one integration.  To keep track of the order, fix $k$ and write
$A_i=A_{k,i}$.  A word of length $n$ means the ordered product
$A_{i_1}\cdots A_{i_n}$, where each $i_r$ lies in $[k]=\{1,\ldots,k\}$.
Its support is the set of indices that occur.  Call the word complete
when its support is $[k]$.

For example, in dimension two, direct integration gives
\[
 (A_1A_2f)(x_1,x_2)
 =\int_{\Delta_2}\mathbf1_{y_1+x_2\leq1}f(y_1,y_2)\,dy_1dy_2.
\]
The kernel is bounded by one.  Thus this product is compact by
Lemma~\ref{lem:kernel}, although integration in just one coordinate
retains arbitrary dependence on the other coordinate.

\begin{lemma}\label{all:lem:complete-words}
Every complete word has a measurable integral kernel on
$\Delta_k\times\Delta_k$ with values in $[0,1]$.
It is compact, with norm at most $1/k!$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Extend a bounded $f$ by zero to the unit cube.
Start with $x^{(0)}=x$ and replace coordinate $i_r$ by $t_r\in[0,1]$
to obtain $x^{(r)}$.  Successive integration gives
\[
 (A_{i_1}\cdots A_{i_n}f)(x)
 =\int_{[0,1]^n}
   \left(\prod_{r=1}^n\mathbf1_{\Delta_k}(x^{(r)})\right)
   f(x^{(n)})\,dt_1\cdots dt_n.
\]
For each coordinate $j$, let $r_j$ be its last occurrence in the word.
The final coordinate is $y_j=t_{r_j}$.
The $r_j$ are distinct, so this selects $k$ distinct integration variables.
Rename the other $n-k$ variables as $v$.
This is a permutation of variables, with absolute Jacobian one.
Fubini gives the kernel
\begin{equation}\label{all:eq:complete-kernel}
 h(x,y)=\int_{[0,1]^{n-k}}
        \prod_{r=1}^n\mathbf1_{\Delta_k}(x^{(r)}(x,y,v))\,dv.
\end{equation}
The final indicator restricts $y$ to $\Delta_k$.
The kernel is measurable and lies in $[0,1]$.
When $n=k$, the integral means the product of indicators with no variables
remaining.  Lemma~\ref{lem:kernel} gives the norm bound and compactness.
Density of bounded functions in $H_k$ extends the identity to every $f$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Fixing coordinates}

A sum over only some coordinates acts separately on each slice of the
others.  Let $\varnothing\ne S\subsetneq[k]$, write $m=|S|$, and put
$A_S=\sum_{i\in S}A_i$.
List the coordinates in $S$ and its complement in increasing order.
Write $x$ for the first group and $w\in\Delta_{k-m}$ for the second.
The slice in $x$ is $a\Delta_m$, where $a=1-\sum_jw_j$.
For almost every $w$, one has $a>0$.
The change of variables
\begin{equation}\label{all:eq:slice-map}
 (\mathcal U_Sf)(u,w)=a^{m/2}f(au,w)
\end{equation}
is an isometric isomorphism
$H_k\to L^2(\Delta_m\times\Delta_{k-m},du\,dw)$.
Indeed, the Jacobian is $a^m$, and the inverse is
$f(x,w)=a^{-m/2}(\mathcal U_Sf)(x/a,w)$.
The coordinate permutation used for the two groups preserves Lebesgue measure.
Changing one line-integration variable gives
\begin{equation}\label{all:eq:slice-operator}
 \mathcal U_S A_S\mathcal U_S^{-1}g(u,w)
       =a\bigl(K_mg(\cdot,w)\bigr)(u).
\end{equation}
There is one factor of $a$ from the length of each integrating line.

\begin{lemma}\label{all:lem:subset-norm}
For every such $S$, $\|A_S\|=M_m$.
Consequently $M_k\geq M_m$ whenever $1\leq m<k$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lem:spectral} identifies $M_m$ with $\|K_m\|$.
Since $0\leq a\leq1$, equation~\eqref{all:eq:slice-operator} gives
$\|A_S\|\leq M_m$.
Choose a unit $u_0\in H_m$ with
$\langle u_0,K_mu_0\rangle>M_m-\eta$.
Choose a unit $q\in L^2(\Delta_{k-m})$ supported where
$\sum_jw_j<\delta$, a set of positive measure.
The function $g(u,w)=u_0(u)q(w)$ is a unit vector, and its quadratic value
under the conjugated operator exceeds $(1-\delta)(M_m-\eta)$.
Let $\delta,\eta\downarrow0$ to prove equality.
Finally, $K_k-A_S$ is a sum of positive operators, so $M_k\geq M_m$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Families of slices}

Let $C_n$ be the sum of the complete words of length $n$.
It is compact by Lemma~\ref{all:lem:complete-words}.
Inclusion--exclusion of the supports of ordered words gives, for $n\geq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{all:eq:word-sum}
 K_k^n-C_n
 =\sum_{\varnothing\ne S\subsetneq[k]}(-1)^{k-|S|+1}A_S^n.
\end{equation}
To check the coefficient of a word with support of size $r<k$, sum over
all proper sets containing that support.  Its coefficient is
\[
 \sum_{j=0}^{k-r-1}(-1)^{k-r-j+1}\binom{k-r}{j}=1,
\]
because the full alternating binomial sum vanishes.
A complete word occurs in none of the proper subsets.
No factors have been commuted in this argument.
Lemma~\ref{all:lem:subset-norm} now gives
\begin{equation}\label{all:eq:essential-bound}
 \|K_k^n-C_n\|
 \leq\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}\binom{k}{m}M_m^n
 \leq(2^k-2)M_{k-1}^n.
\end{equation}
The sum $C_n$ is self-adjoint by \eqref{all:eq:word-sum}.
An individual complete word need not be self-adjoint or positive as a
quadratic form, despite its nonnegative kernel.

\begin{proposition}\label{all:prop:essential}
For $k\geq2$, one has $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(K_k)\subset[0,M_{k-1}]$.
If $K_{k-1}$ has a unit eigenfunction at $M_{k-1}$, then
\[
                    \sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(K_k)=[0,M_{k-1}].
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $f_j$ be a singular sequence at $\lambda$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:spine-operator} gives $0\leq\lambda\leq k$.
For every fixed $n$,
\[
 K_k^n-\lambda^nI=(K_k-\lambda I)
                    \sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\lambda^rK_k^{n-1-r}
\]
shows that $(K_k^n-\lambda^nI)f_j\to0$ in norm.
Also $C_nf_j\to0$ by compactness.
Applying \eqref{all:eq:essential-bound} and then letting $j\to\infty$ gives
$\lambda^n\leq(2^k-2)M_{k-1}^n$.
Take $n$th roots and then let $n\to\infty$.
The conclusion is $\lambda\leq M_{k-1}$.

For the reverse inclusion, put $d=k-1$ and suppose
$K_d\psi=M_d\psi$, $\|\psi\|=1$.
Fix $z_0\in[0,1]$ and set $\lambda=(1-z_0)M_d$.
Choose intervals $E_j\subset(0,1)$ of positive lengths $\varepsilon_j\to0$
with $\delta_j=\sup_{z\in E_j}|z-z_0|\to0$.
Writing $x\in(1-z)\Delta_d$, define
\begin{equation}\label{all:eq:concentration}
 f_j(x,z)=\varepsilon_j^{-1/2}\mathbf1_{E_j}(z)
              (1-z)^{-d/2}\psi\left(\frac{x}{1-z}\right).
\end{equation}
The isometry \eqref{all:eq:slice-map} gives $\|f_j\|=1$.
For any $g\in H_k$,
$|\langle g,f_j\rangle|\leq\|\mathbf1_{z\in E_j}g\|\to0$
by absolute continuity of the integral of $|g|^2$.
Equation~\eqref{all:eq:slice-operator} gives
\[
 \left\|\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}A_{k,i}-\lambda I\right)f_j\right\|
 \leq M_d\delta_j.
\]
The last line integral is supported, in its integrating variable, on a
set of length at most $\varepsilon_j$.
Cauchy--Schwarz therefore gives
$\|T_{k,k}f_j\|^2\leq\varepsilon_j\|f_j\|^2=\varepsilon_j$.
Since $\|T_{k,k}^*\|\leq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{all:eq:concentration-error}
 \|(K_k-\lambda I)f_j\|\leq M_d\delta_j+\sqrt{\varepsilon_j}\to0.
\end{equation}
This includes $z_0=0$, where mass approaches a face, and $z_0=1$,
where the slices shrink to a vertex.
The amplitude in \eqref{all:eq:concentration} is exactly compensated by the
slice measure, so no boundary value of $\psi$ is required.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Changing the slice}

To raise the quadratic quotient, we shall use slices close to the face
$z=0$ and retain the contribution of integration in $z$.
We must compare an arbitrary $L^2$ function with its values after a change
of scale.
For $0<a\leq1$ and $u\in H_d$, define
\begin{equation}\label{all:eq:dilation}
 (D_au)(x)=a^{-d/2}u(x/a)\mathbf1_{a\Delta_d}(x),\qquad D_a:H_d\to H_d.
\end{equation}
The map $D_a$ is called a dilation.
The function $u$ is extended by zero outside $\Delta_d$.
Change of variables gives $\|D_au\|=\|u\|$.
For $a<1$ this is an isometric embedding, rather than an isomorphism onto
all of $H_d$.

We first justify the smooth approximation needed for this map.
The space $C_c^\infty(\Delta_d^\circ)$ consists of smooth functions whose
support is a compact subset of the interior.
It is dense in $H_d$.
To see this, restrict $u$ to the compact sets
\[
 E_j=\{x:x_i\geq1/j\text{ for all }i,\ \sum_i x_i\leq1-1/j\}.
\]
Their union is the interior, so $u\mathbf1_{E_j}\to u$ in $L^2$.
Translation is continuous in $L^2(\mathbb R^d)$: this holds for indicators
of bounded boxes because the measure of their symmetric difference with
a small translate tends to zero.
Approximation by finite sums of such indicators proves the assertion for
all $L^2$ functions.

Choose a nonnegative smooth function $\eta$ supported in the Euclidean
unit ball and with integral one.
For example, normalize $\exp(-1/(1-\sum_i x_i^2))$ inside that ball,
with value zero outside.
Put $\eta_\delta(x)=\delta^{-d}\eta(x/\delta)$ and
$g_\delta(x)=\int\eta_\delta(h)(u\mathbf1_{E_j})(x-h)\,dh$.
For small enough $\delta$, this is smooth with compact support in the
simplex interior.  Cauchy--Schwarz against the measure
$\eta_\delta(h)\,dh$ gives
\[
 \|g_\delta-u\mathbf1_{E_j}\|_2^2
 \leq\int\eta_\delta(h)
       \|(u\mathbf1_{E_j})(\cdot-h)-u\mathbf1_{E_j}\|_2^2\,dh\to0.
\]
Translation continuity proves the last limit and hence the asserted density.

\begin{lemma}\label{all:lem:dilation-continuity}
For every $u\in H_d$, $D_au\to u$ in $H_d$ as $a\uparrow1$.
If $\|\psi\|=1$, choose $g\in C_c^\infty(\Delta_d^\circ)$ with
$\|\psi-g\|<1/8$, and set
\begin{equation}\label{all:eq:epsilon}
 B_g=\left\|\frac d2g+\sum_{i=1}^d x_i\partial_i g\right\|_2,
 \qquad \varepsilon=\frac1{8(1+B_g)}.
\end{equation}
Then $\|D_{1-z}\psi-\psi\|<1/2$ for $0\leq z\leq\varepsilon$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The zero extension of $g$ is smooth on $\mathbb R^d$.
Differentiate its dilation to obtain
\[
 \frac{d}{da}D_ag
 =-a^{-1}D_a\left(\frac d2g+\sum_i x_i\partial_i g\right).
\]
Integrating from $a$ to $1$ and using the isometry gives
$\|D_ag-g\|\leq B_g\log(1/a)$.
The integral estimate follows by testing against unit vectors and applying
Fubini, or directly by Cauchy--Schwarz to the parameter integral.
Approximation now gives
\[
 \|D_au-u\|\leq2\|u-g\|+B_g\log(1/a),
\]
which proves continuity by choosing $g$ first and then $a$.
For the specified $g$, one has $\varepsilon\leq1/8$ and
$-\log(1-z)\leq z/(1-z)\leq2z$ on $[0,\varepsilon]$.
Thus
\[
 \|D_{1-z}\psi-\psi\|
 \leq2\|\psi-g\|+2zB_g
 <\frac14+\frac{B_g}{4(1+B_g)}<\frac12.
\]
Only the smooth function $g$ was differentiated.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Adding one variable}

Suppose $d\geq1$ and $K_d\psi=M_d\psi$, with $\|\psi\|=1$.
For $q\in L^2(0,1)$ define a function on the actual $(d+1)$-simplex by
\begin{equation}\label{all:eq:lift}
 F_q(x,z)=q(z)D_{1-z}\psi(x),\qquad x\in(1-z)\Delta_d.
\end{equation}
We call $q$ the profile in the new coordinate.
The map is measurable for a measurable representative of $\psi$.
The zero extension makes its support agree with the simplex.
Fubini and the isometry give $\|F_q\|^2=\int_0^1q(z)^2\,dz$.
For $i\leq d$, put $a=1-z$ and change the integrating variable:
\[
 T_{d+1,i}F_q(x_{\widehat i},z)
 =q(z)a^{1-d/2}(T_{d,i}\psi)(x_{\widehat i}/a).
\]
Integration over $x_{\widehat i}$ contributes the Jacobian $a^{d-1}$.
Consequently
\[
 \sum_{i=1}^d\|T_{d+1,i}F_q\|^2
       =M_d\int_0^1(1-z)q(z)^2\,dz.
\]
The last line integral is the $H_d$ function
\[
 I_q(x)=T_{d+1,d+1}F_q(x)
       =\int_0^1q(z)D_{1-z}\psi(x)\,dz.
\]
This function belongs to $H_d$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:simplex}.
The integral is interpreted almost everywhere through this displayed
line integral.  Fubini permits testing it against any $v\in H_d$, since
\[
 \int_0^1 |q(z)|\int_{\Delta_d}|v(x)D_{1-z}\psi(x)|\,dx\,dz
 \leq\|v\|\int_0^1|q(z)|\,dz<\infty.
\]
We therefore obtain the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{all:eq:excess}
 \langle F_q,K_{d+1}F_q\rangle-M_d\|F_q\|^2
       =\|I_q\|^2-M_d\int_0^1zq(z)^2\,dz.
\end{equation}
It separates the gain from integrating in the new coordinate from the
loss caused by shorter slices.

\begin{proposition}\label{all:prop:strict-increase}
Under this eigenfunction assumption, choose $g$ and $\varepsilon$ as in
Lemma~\ref{all:lem:dilation-continuity}.  Then
\begin{equation}\label{all:eq:positive-gap}
 M_{d+1}-M_d\geq\frac{8\varepsilon d^2}{e^{8d}-1}>0.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For nonnegative $q$ supported in $(0,\varepsilon]$, the dilation estimate
and the preceding Fubini identity give
\[
 \langle\psi,I_q\rangle
 =\int q(z)\langle\psi,D_{1-z}\psi\rangle\,dz
 \geq\frac12\int q(z)\,dz.
\]
Since $\|\psi\|=1$, it follows that $\|I_q\|\geq\tfrac12\int q$.
Set $L=8d$, $\alpha=\varepsilon e^{-L}$, and choose
\[
 q(z)=z^{-1}\mathbf1_{[\alpha,\varepsilon]}(z).
\]
The positive lower endpoint ensures $q\in L^2(0,1)$.
Direct integration gives
\begin{equation}\label{all:eq:profile-integrals}
 \int q=L,\qquad \int zq^2=L,\qquad
 \int q^2=\frac{e^L-1}{\varepsilon}.
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{all:eq:excess} and~\eqref{eq:spine-operator} imply
\[
 \langle F_q,K_{d+1}F_q\rangle-M_d\|F_q\|^2
 \geq\frac{L^2}{4}-M_dL
 \geq16d^2-8d^2=8d^2.
\]
Divide by the positive mass in \eqref{all:eq:profile-integrals} to prove
\eqref{all:eq:positive-gap}.
The constant depends on the chosen smooth approximation to $\psi$.
It requires no boundary regularity of $\psi$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The maximal quotient}

\begin{theorem}\label{all:thm:all-dimensions}
For every finite integer $k\geq2$,
\[
 \sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(K_k)=[0,M_{k-1}],\qquad M_k>M_{k-1}.
\]
The number $M_k$ is an isolated simple eigenvalue.
There is a unique unit eigenfunction $\Phi_k$ at $M_k$ that is positive
almost everywhere.  It is invariant under every coordinate permutation.
For $k=1$, $M_1=1$ and $\Phi_1=1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The base operator is
$K_1f=(\int_0^1f(t)\,dt)1$, which has norm one and unit eigenfunction $1$.
Suppose a unit eigenfunction at $M_d$ has been obtained.
Proposition~\ref{all:prop:essential} gives the full essential interval for
$K_{d+1}$.  Proposition~\ref{all:prop:strict-increase} gives
$M_{d+1}>M_d$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:spectral} supplies an isolated top eigenvalue of finite
multiplicity in dimension $d+1$.
This induction establishes existence, the essential interval, and the
strict dimension inequality for each finite dimension.

Fix $k$ and take a real top eigenfunction $f\ne0$.
Replacing $f$ by $|f|$ does not decrease any squared line integral, and
preserves its norm.  Maximality makes $|f|$ another maximizer.
The residual estimate in Lemma~\ref{lem:spectral} makes it an eigenfunction.
Normalize it to obtain a nonnegative unit eigenfunction $\Phi_k$.

For strict positivity, use the complete word
$L=(A_1\cdots A_k)^2$.
For interior $x,y$, put
\[
 \eta=\frac{\min(1-\sum_i x_i,\ 1-\sum_i y_i)}{2k}>0.
\]
In the kernel \eqref{all:eq:complete-kernel}, the first $k$ replacements
change $x$ into $v$, and the next $k$ change $v$ into $y$.
If every $v_i$ lies in $(0,\eta)$, the sum at a point on the first
part of the path is at most $\sum_i x_i+k\eta<1$.
On the second part it is at most $\sum_i y_i+k\eta<1$.
All coordinates remain nonnegative.
Thus the kernel of $L$ is at least $\eta^k>0$ for every interior pair.
Since $K_k^{2k}$ contains $L$ among its nonnegativity-preserving summands,
it sends each nonzero nonnegative function to a positive function almost
everywhere.  The identity $\Phi_k=M_k^{-2k}K_k^{2k}\Phi_k$ proves
$\Phi_k>0$ almost everywhere.

Suppose a real top eigenfunction $h$ changes sign.
Write $h=h_+-h_-$, where $h_+=\max(h,0)$ and $h_-=\max(-h,0)$.
Both parts are nonzero.  The positive kernel just constructed gives
$\langle h_+,K_k^{2k}h_-\rangle>0$.
Self-adjointness implies
\[
 \langle |h|,K_k^{2k}|h|\rangle-\langle h,K_k^{2k}h\rangle
 =4\langle h_+,K_k^{2k}h_-\rangle>0.
\]
But the second quadratic value equals $M_k^{2k}\|h\|^2$, whereas
$\|K_k^{2k}\|\leq M_k^{2k}$.  This is impossible.
Two independent top eigenfunctions would give a nonzero one orthogonal
to $\Phi_k$.  Such a function must change sign, so the eigenspace is simple.

For a permutation $\sigma$ of $[k]$, define
$(U_\sigma f)(t)=f(t_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,t_{\sigma(k)})$.
These maps preserve the simplex and Lebesgue measure.
They are isometries and satisfy $U_\sigma U_\tau=U_{\sigma\tau}$,
where $(\sigma\tau)(i)=\sigma(\tau(i))$.
Changing a line-integration variable shows
$U_\sigma A_iU_\sigma^{-1}=A_{\sigma(i)}$.
Hence $U_\sigma$ commutes with $K_k$.
The positive unit eigenfunction $U_\sigma\Phi_k$ must equal $\Phi_k$ by
simplicity, proving symmetry and uniqueness of the positive normalization.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{all:cor:compact-distance}
For every finite $k\geq2$ and every compact $C:H_k\to H_k$,
\[
                         \|K_k-C\|\geq M_{k-1}>0.
\]
In particular, finite-rank operators cannot converge to $K_k$ in operator norm.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Use the singular sequence at $M_{k-1}$ from
Proposition~\ref{all:prop:essential} and Theorem~\ref{all:thm:all-dimensions}.
Its image under $C$ tends to zero, while
$K_kf_j-M_{k-1}f_j\to0$ in norm.  Apply $K_k-C$ to its unit vectors.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A constant profile near a face]\label{all:ex:flat-profile}
A constant profile behaves differently from the one in
Proposition~\ref{all:prop:strict-increase}.
Take $q_\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{-1/2}\mathbf1_{(0,\varepsilon)}$ in
\eqref{all:eq:lift}, with $\psi=\Phi_d$.
Dilation continuity gives
\[
 \left\|I_{q_\varepsilon}-\sqrt{\varepsilon}\psi\right\|
 \leq\sqrt{\varepsilon}\sup_{0<z<\varepsilon}
                         \|D_{1-z}\psi-\psi\|=o(\sqrt{\varepsilon}).
\]
Here $o(\sqrt{\varepsilon})$ means a quantity whose ratio to
$\sqrt{\varepsilon}$ tends to zero as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$.
The mass is one and $\int zq_\varepsilon^2=\varepsilon/2$.
Therefore
\[
 \langle F_{q_\varepsilon},K_{d+1}F_{q_\varepsilon}\rangle-M_d
       =\varepsilon\left(1-\frac{M_d}{2}+o(1)\right).
\]
If $M_d>2$, this quantity is negative for all sufficiently small
$\varepsilon$.
For example, \eqref{all:eq:linear-bound} gives $M_7\geq21/10>2$.
Thus a constant profile applied to the actual seventh-dimensional top
eigenfunction does not prove a strict increase to dimension eight.
The nonconstant profile in \eqref{all:eq:profile-integrals} does.
For the base function $\Phi_1=1$, the dilation is explicitly
$D_a1=a^{-1/2}\mathbf1_{[0,a]}$, and
$\langle D_a1,1\rangle=\sqrt a$, consistent with the continuity estimate.
\end{example}

\subsection{Polynomial functions}

The eigenfunction also has a specified construction by repeated coordinate
integration.  For each fixed $k$, put
$p_{k,0}=1$ and $p_{k,n+1}=K_kp_{k,n}$.
These are the unnormalized iterates.  Their normalization means division
by their $L^2$ norm.

\begin{theorem}\label{all:thm:iteration}
For every fixed $k\geq1$,
\[
 \frac{p_{k,n}}{\|p_{k,n}\|}\longrightarrow\Phi_k
                 \quad\text{in }H_k,
 \qquad
 \frac{\langle p_{k,n},K_kp_{k,n}\rangle}{\|p_{k,n}\|^2}
                  \longrightarrow M_k.
\]
The unnormalized $p_{k,n}$ are symmetric polynomials with rational
coefficients.  Their normalized forms need not have rational coefficients.
For $k\geq2$ there are $c_k>0$, $0\leq r_k<M_k$, and $h_k\perp\Phi_k$
such that
\begin{equation}\label{all:eq:iteration-error}
 1=c_k\Phi_k+h_k,\qquad
 \|M_k^{-n}p_{k,n}-c_k\Phi_k\|
            \leq(r_k/M_k)^n\|h_k\|\quad(n\geq1).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $k=1$ all iterates equal $1$.
For $k\geq2$, self-adjointness makes $\Phi_k^\perp$ invariant.
The restriction of $K_k$ is positive and has norm $r_k\leq M_k$.
If equality held, Lemma~\ref{lem:spectral} would give unit vectors
$g_j\perp\Phi_k$ with $(K_k-M_kI)g_j\to0$.
A weak subsequential limit would lie in the top eigenspace and its
orthogonal complement, hence would be zero.
This would be a singular sequence at $M_k>M_{k-1}$, contradicting
Theorem~\ref{all:thm:all-dimensions}.  Thus $r_k<M_k$.

Set $c_k=\langle1,\Phi_k\rangle>0$ and $h_k=1-c_k\Phi_k$.
Their orthogonal decomposition gives
$M_k^{-n}p_{k,n}=c_k\Phi_k+M_k^{-n}K_k^nh_k$.
The second term has norm at most $(r_k/M_k)^n\|h_k\|$.
It tends to zero, proving convergence after normalization.
The component $c_kM_k^n\Phi_k$ also shows that every $p_{k,n}$ is nonzero.
Continuity of the quadratic form proves convergence of the Rayleigh values.

For nonnegative integers $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_k$,
\begin{equation}\label{all:eq:monomial-action}
 A_{k,i}\left(\prod_jt_j^{\alpha_j}\right)
 =\frac{\prod_{j\ne i}t_j^{\alpha_j}}{\alpha_i+1}
                  \left(1-\sum_{j\ne i}t_j\right)^{\alpha_i+1}.
\end{equation}
Thus each coordinate integral preserves rational polynomial coefficients.
Their sum commutes with coordinate permutations, so it preserves symmetry.
Starting from $1$ proves the assertions about the unnormalized iterates.
\end{proof}

Their squared norms and quadratic values can be integrated exactly:
\begin{equation}\label{all:eq:monomial-integral}
 \int_{\Delta_k}\prod_jt_j^{\alpha_j}\,dt
      =\frac{\prod_j\alpha_j!}{(k+\sum_j\alpha_j)!}.
\end{equation}
For two exponents, integration by parts gives
$\int_0^1t^a(1-t)^b\,dt=a!b!/(a+b+1)!$ by induction on $b$.
Successive integration and this identity prove
\eqref{all:eq:monomial-integral} in every dimension.

\begin{example}[Normalization in dimension two]
The\label{all:ex:normalization} first unnormalized iterate is $p_{2,1}=2-x-y$.
Integrating over lines $x+y=s$ gives
\[
 \|p_{2,1}\|^2=\int_0^1s(2-s)^2\,ds=\frac{11}{12},\qquad
 \frac{p_{2,1}}{\|p_{2,1}\|}=\sqrt{\frac{12}{11}}(2-x-y).
\]
The square root is irrational.  If it were a reduced fraction $p/q$,
then $11p^2=12q^2$ would force $11\mid q$ and then $11\mid p$.
This contradicts coprimality.
Thus normalization does not preserve rational coefficients.
\end{example}

All assertions above concern one finite dimension at a time.
The positive difference in \eqref{all:eq:positive-gap} depends on a smooth
approximation to a lower-dimensional eigenfunction.
The number $r_k$ in \eqref{all:eq:iteration-error} describes a different gap,
within a fixed dimension, and no numerical value for it is given.
Exact values of $M_k$, boundary regularity of $\Phi_k$, and growth as
$k$ tends to infinity remain separate questions.


\section{Quadratic polynomial bounds}\label{var:section}

The maximal eigenfunction gives an intrinsic description of $M_k$.
Explicit polynomials give lower bounds that can be evaluated using rational
arithmetic.  We will compute five such bounds, in dimensions eight through
twelve, and then preserve their strict inequalities under smooth cutoff.

Fix $k\geq2$ and use the spaces and operators of
Proposition~\ref{prop:simplex}.  To keep the dimension visible, recall
\begin{equation}\label{var:eq:T}
 T_{k,i}:H_k\longrightarrow H_{k-1},\qquad
 (T_{k,i}f)(x)=\int_0^{\ell_i(x)}f(x_1,\ldots,u,\ldots,x_k)\,du.
\end{equation}
Here $x=t_{\widehat i}$ and $\ell_i(x)=1-\sum_{j\ne i}x_j$;
the remaining coordinates retain their indices.
The fibrewise Cauchy--Schwarz inequality gives
\begin{equation}\label{var:eq:bounded}
 \norm{T_{k,i}f}^2
 \leq\int_{\Delta_k}\ell_i(t_{\widehat i})|f(t)|^2\,dt
 \leq\norm f^2.
\end{equation}
Fubini gives $T_{k,i}^*g(t)=g(t_{\widehat i})$.  Thus
\begin{equation}\label{var:eq:K}
 A_{k,i}=T_{k,i}^*T_{k,i},\qquad K_k=\sum_{i=1}^kA_{k,i},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{var:eq:energy}
 \ip f{K_kg}=\sum_i\ip{T_{k,i}f}{T_{k,i}g},\qquad
 0\leq\ip f{K_kf}\leq k\norm f^2.
\end{equation}
The two quantities $\norm f^2$ and $\ip f{K_kf}$ are its mass and energy.
For nonzero $f$, write
\begin{equation}\label{var:eq:variational}
 R_k(f)=\frac{\ip f{K_kf}}{\norm f^2},\qquad
 M_k=\sup_{f\ne0}R_k(f)=\norm{K_k}.
\end{equation}
Multiplying $f$ by a nonzero real constant leaves its quotient unchanged.
Symmetric polynomials of degree at most two have four coefficients.
Their masses and energies can be computed by integrating monomials.

\subsection{Integrating monomials}

A multiindex $\alpha=(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_k)$ is a tuple of
nonnegative integers.  Write
$|\alpha|=\sum_i\alpha_i$, $t^\alpha=\prod_i t_i^{\alpha_i}$, and
$\alpha!=\prod_i\alpha_i!$, where $0!=1$.
The slack variable is $1-\sum_i t_i$.
We need its mixed moments with coordinate monomials.

\begin{lemma}
For\label{var:lem:moment} every multiindex $\alpha$ and integer $b\geq0$,
\begin{equation}\label{var:eq:moment}
 \int_{\Delta_k}t^\alpha\left(1-\sum_i t_i\right)^b\,dt
       =\frac{\alpha!\,b!}{(k+|\alpha|+b)!}.
\end{equation}
In particular, the simplex has measure $1/k!$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
First, for integers $a,b\geq0$, integration by parts gives
\[
 \int_0^1u^a(1-u)^b\,du=\frac{a!b!}{(a+b+1)!}.
\]
For $b=0$, the integral is $1/(a+1)$.
For $b>0$, integration by parts replaces the integral by
$b/(a+1)$ times the integral with exponents $a+1,b-1$.
Iteration proves the formula.
For a fixed sum $v=\sum_{i<k}t_i$, substitute $t_k=(1-v)u$.
Integration in $t_k$ then gives
\[
 \frac{\alpha_k!b!}{(\alpha_k+b+1)!}
                   (1-v)^{\alpha_k+b+1}.
\]
Apply the same calculation to the remaining $k-1$ variables.
The intermediate factorials cancel, leaving \eqref{var:eq:moment}.
\end{proof}

For $f=1$, every coordinate integral is $\ell_i$.
For $f=1-s$, where $s=\sum_i t_i$, every coordinate integral is
$\ell_i^2/2$.  Equation~\eqref{var:eq:moment} therefore gives
\begin{equation}\label{var:eq:elementary}
 R_k(1)=\frac{2k}{k+1},\qquad R_k(1-s)=\frac{3k}{k+3}.
\end{equation}
For example, the latter mass is $2/(k+2)!$, and its energy is
$k\cdot4!/[4(k+3)!]=6k/(k+3)!$.
The different factorials arise because a coordinate integral lives in
one fewer dimension.

We now compute the two bilinear forms on arbitrary monomials.
Let $G_{\alpha\beta}=\ip{t^\alpha}{t^\beta}$ and
$B_{\alpha\beta}=\ip{t^\alpha}{K_kt^\beta}$.
A matrix of inner products such as $G$ is called a Gram matrix.
A real symmetric matrix $G$ is positive definite when $c^{\mathsf T}Gc>0$
for every nonzero real coefficient column $c$.  The superscript
$\mathsf T$ denotes transpose.

\begin{proposition}
For\label{var:prop:monomial} multiindices $\alpha,\beta$ of length $k$,
\begin{align}
 G_{\alpha\beta}
 &=\frac{\prod_j(\alpha_j+\beta_j)!}{(k+|\alpha|+|\beta|)!},
 \label{var:eq:G}\\
 B_{\alpha\beta}
 &=\frac{1}{(k+|\alpha|+|\beta|+1)!}
   \sum_{i=1}^k
   \frac{(\alpha_i+\beta_i+2)!\prod_{j\ne i}(\alpha_j+\beta_j)!}
        {(\alpha_i+1)(\beta_i+1)}.
 \label{var:eq:B}
\end{align}
Both matrices are symmetric.  Every finite Gram matrix $G$ indexed by
distinct monomials is positive definite.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{var:eq:G} is \eqref{var:eq:moment} with $b=0$.
Direct integration in the $i$th coordinate gives
\begin{equation}\label{var:eq:monomial-action}
 T_{k,i}t^\alpha
 =\frac{\prod_{j\ne i}t_j^{\alpha_j}}{\alpha_i+1}
                     \ell_i^{\alpha_i+1}.
\end{equation}
Multiply the expressions for $\alpha$ and $\beta$.
Apply \eqref{var:eq:moment} in dimension $k-1$, with slack exponent
$\alpha_i+\beta_i+2$.
Its denominator is
$(k-1+\sum_{j\ne i}(\alpha_j+\beta_j)+\alpha_i+\beta_i+2)!$,
which is the denominator in \eqref{var:eq:B}.
Summing over $i$ proves the formula by \eqref{var:eq:energy}.

Symmetry is visible in both formulas.
If a polynomial had zero squared integral, continuity would make it
zero on the simplex interior.
A polynomial that vanishes on an open box is identically zero:
fix all but one coordinate and use that a nonzero univariate polynomial
has finitely many roots, then repeat in the other coordinates.
Distinct monomials have independent coefficients, proving positive
definiteness.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Symmetric quadratic functions}

A partition $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)$ is a nonincreasing
list of positive integers.  Its length is $r$ and its degree is
$|\lambda|=\sum_j\lambda_j$.
When $r\leq k$, let $\mathcal O_k(\lambda)$ be the set of distinct
permutations of $(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r,0,\ldots,0)$, and define
\[
 m_\lambda(t)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal O_k(\lambda)}t^\alpha.
\]
For the empty partition, $m_{\varnothing}=1$.
If $a_j$ is the number of parts of $\lambda$ equal to $j$, then
\begin{equation}\label{var:eq:orbit-size}
 |\mathcal O_k(\lambda)|=\frac{k!}{(k-r)!\prod_{j\geq1}a_j!}.
\end{equation}
Choose distinct positions for the $r$ parts, then divide by the
permutations of equal parts.  Thus every monomial occurs exactly once.
A symmetric polynomial has constant coefficients on these orbits.
For each integer $d\geq0$, the $m_\lambda$ of degree at most $d$
therefore form a basis of the
symmetric polynomials of degree at most $d$.

Write $\mathsf G_{\lambda\mu}=\ip{m_\lambda}{m_\mu}$ and
$\mathsf B_{\lambda\mu}=\ip{m_\lambda}{K_km_\mu}$.
Fix $\alpha_\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r,0,\ldots,0)$.
Simultaneous coordinate permutation leaves \eqref{var:eq:G} and
\eqref{var:eq:B} unchanged.  Hence
\begin{equation}\label{var:eq:orbit-sum}
 \mathsf G_{\lambda\mu}
  =|\mathcal O_k(\lambda)|\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal O_k(\mu)}
                             G_{\alpha_\lambda\beta},\qquad
 \mathsf B_{\lambda\mu}
  =|\mathcal O_k(\lambda)|\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal O_k(\mu)}
                             B_{\alpha_\lambda\beta}.
\end{equation}
These sums give the full combinatorial multiplicities.
For a coefficient column $c$, the polynomial $f=\sum_\lambda c_\lambda m_\lambda$
has quotient
\begin{equation}\label{var:eq:matrix-quotient}
 R_k(f)=\frac{c^{\mathsf T}\mathsf Bc}{c^{\mathsf T}\mathsf Gc}.
\end{equation}
For nonzero $c$, the denominator is positive by
Proposition~\ref{var:prop:monomial}.
Rational coefficients therefore give a rational quotient.
No normalization of either matrix or simplex measure is implicit.

For degree at most two, use the ordered basis
\begin{equation}\label{var:eq:basis}
 b_0=1,\quad b_1=s=\sum_i t_i,\quad
 b_2=p_2=\sum_i t_i^2,\quad
 b_3=e_2=\sum_{i<j}t_it_j.
\end{equation}
These are $m_{\varnothing},m_{(1)},m_{(2)},m_{(1,1)}$.
Their degrees are $d_0=0,d_1=1,d_2=d_3=2$.
The relation $s^2=p_2+2e_2$ will be useful below.
Define integers $N_{ab}(k)$ and $P_{ab}(k)$ by
\begin{equation}\label{var:eq:quadratic-matrices}
 \mathsf G_{ab}=\frac{N_{ab}(k)}{(k+d_a+d_b)!},\qquad
 \mathsf B_{ab}=\frac{P_{ab}(k)}{(k+d_a+d_b+1)!}.
\end{equation}
Both arrays are symmetric.  Their entries are
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccll}
\toprule
$a$&$b$&$N_{ab}(k)$&$P_{ab}(k)$\\
\midrule
0&0&$1$&$2k$\\
0&1&$k$&$k(2k+1)$\\
0&2&$2k$&$4k(k+1)$\\
0&3&$k(k-1)/2$&$k(k-1)(k+1)$\\
1&1&$k(k+1)$&$2k^2(k+2)$\\
1&2&$2k(k+2)$&$2k(2k^2+7k+1)$\\
1&3&$k(k-1)(k+2)/2$&$k(k-1)(2k^2+7k+2)/2$\\
2&2&$4k(k+5)$&$8k(k^2+7k+2)$\\
2&3&$k(k-1)(k+4)$&$2k(k-1)(k^2+6k+3)$\\
3&3&$k(k-1)(k^2+3k-2)/4$&$k(k-1)(k^3+5k^2-4)/2$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Here is the counting argument behind the table.
For $b_1^2$, equal coordinate indices contribute $2k$ to $N_{11}$,
and unequal indices contribute $k(k-1)$.
For $P_{11}$, these contributions are $k(4k+2)$ and
$k(k-1)(2k+2)$, respectively, by \eqref{var:eq:B}.
This gives the row $a=b=1$.
For the last row, fix the pair supporting a monomial of $e_2$.
The second pair can be equal, intersect in one index, or be disjoint.
There are $1$, $2(k-2)$, and $(k-2)(k-3)/2$ choices, respectively.
The corresponding contributions to $N_{33}$ are $4$, $2$, and $1$.
Their contributions to $P_{33}$ are $8k+8$, $4k+6$, and $2k+4$.
Multiply their weighted sums by $k(k-1)/2$ to obtain the last row.
Every other row uses the same equal-index or disjoint-index count in
\eqref{var:eq:orbit-sum}.

The matrix $\mathsf B$ represents the quadratic form of $K_k$ on this
polynomial subspace.  It is not the coefficient matrix of $K_k$ in
that basis: \eqref{var:eq:monomial-action} can increase the degree.
Only the quotient \eqref{var:eq:matrix-quotient} is needed for the lower bound.

\subsection{Five explicit polynomials}

For integers $k=8,9,10,11,12$, define
\begin{equation}\label{var:eq:trials}
 f_k=100+u_ks+v_kp_2+w_ke_2,
\end{equation}
where the three integer coefficients appear in the following table.
Each polynomial is nonzero because its constant coefficient is $100$.
Define its scaled mass and energy by
\begin{equation}\label{var:eq:scaled}
 D_k=(k+4)!\norm{f_k}^2,\qquad
 E_k=(k+5)!\ip{f_k}{K_kf_k}.
\end{equation}
The factorials clear the denominators in \eqref{var:eq:quadratic-matrices}.

\end{center}

For comparison, define $q_k=K_k^2 1$, the result of applying $K_k$ twice
to the constant function.  These comparison functions have an explicit
quadratic form.  Indeed,
\[
 K_k1=\sum_i\ell_i=k-(k-1)s,\qquad
 A_{k,i}(K_k1)=\ell_i+\frac{k-1}{2}\ell_i^2.
\]
Since
$\sum_i\ell_i^2=k-2(k-1)s+(k-2)s^2+p_2$,
we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{var:eq:K2}
 q_k=\frac{k(k+1)}2-k(k-1)s
                   +\frac{(k-1)^2}{2}p_2+(k-1)(k-2)e_2.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}
For\label{var:thm:bounds} each dimension in the table below,
$M_k\geq R_k(f_k)>R_k(q_k)$, with the indicated exact values.
Moreover, $R_k(f_k)$ exceeds both elementary quotients in
\eqref{var:eq:elementary}.

In particular,

\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $c=(100,u_k,v_k,w_k)^{\mathsf T}$.
The displayed integers in \eqref{var:eq:scaled} result from the finite sums

 D_k&=\sum_{a,b=0}^3 c_ac_bN_{ab}(k)
                  \frac{(k+4)!}{(k+d_a+d_b)!},\label{var:eq:Dsum}\\
 E_k&=\sum_{a,b=0}^3 c_ac_bP_{ab}(k)
                  \frac{(k+5)!}{(k+d_a+d_b+1)!}.
 \label{var:eq:Esum}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{var:eq:matrix-quotient} and~\eqref{var:eq:scaled} give
$R_k(f_k)=E_k/((k+5)D_k)$, proving the middle column.
Substitution of the four coefficients in \eqref{var:eq:K2} into
\eqref{var:eq:matrix-quotient} gives the last column.
The differences between those two columns, in increasing order of $k$, are

 \frac{10186100}{180615053},\quad
 \frac{39359742}{608280127},\quad
 \frac{177742172}{2344847145},\quad
 \frac{105256327}{1313059848},\quad
 \frac{5754784}{62221143}.
\]
All are positive.

from the corresponding middle-column fraction.
After multiplication by the positive denominators, the differences have
numerators $7127,16801,1795,3730,8$, respectively.
Each of these five simpler fractions exceeds $3k/(k+3)$ in its dimension.
For $k\geq8$, one also has $3k/(k+3)>2k/(k+1)$.
This proves the comparisons with \eqref{var:eq:elementary}.

For example, in dimension twelve the complete construction is
\[
 f_{12}=100-200s+118p_2+196e_2
       =100(1-s)^2+20p_2-2s^2.
\]
Its mass is $361440/16!$ and its energy is $15732480/17!$.
Thus
\[
 R_{12}(f_{12})=\frac{15732480}{17\cdot361440}
             =\frac{1928}{753},\qquad
 \frac{1928}{753}-\frac{64}{25}=\frac8{18825}>0.
\]
A test function need not be nonnegative.
At the boundary point $t_1=\cdots=t_{12}=1/12$, this polynomial equals
$20/12-2=-1/3$, whereas at the origin it equals $100$.
Both values persist with their signs at nearby interior points.
The variational domain permits this change of sign.

The trial coefficients are integers, and no square root enters their
Rayleigh quotients.  Unit normalization would instead replace $f_k$ by
$\sqrt{(k+4)!/D_k}\,f_k$, whose coefficients need not be rational.
All the inequalities above concern the specified continuum operator in
one of five fixed finite dimensions.  They do not identify its exact norm
or the largest quotient among all quadratic polynomials.


\subsection{Smooth functions with the same strict inequality}

Coordinate sampling requires functions defined pointwise, and arithmetic
comparisons often require support away from every face.
The polynomial trials can be replaced by explicit smooth functions with
this support property while retaining any strict lower bound below their
quotients.
Let $\mathcal X_k=C_c^\infty(\Delta_k^\circ;\mathbb R)$ denote the smooth
real functions with compact support in the simplex interior, extended by
zero to $\mathbb R^k$.
Use the norm
\[
 \norm F_{\mathcal X}
 =\sup_{t\in[0,1]^k}\left(|F(t)|+\sum_i|\partial_iF(t)|\right).
\]
We construct the required functions and bound this norm.

Define
\[
 h(x)=\begin{cases}e^{-1/x},&x>0,\\0,&x\leq0,\end{cases}
 \qquad
 \chi(x)=\frac{h(x-1)}{h(x-1)+h(2-x)}.
\]
The denominator is positive for every real $x$.
Every derivative of $h$ from the right is a finite sum of powers of
$x^{-1}$ times $e^{-1/x}$, and tends to zero at the origin.
Indeed, $y^ne^{-y}\to0$ follows from
$e^y\geq y^{n+1}/(n+1)!$ for $y>0$.
Thus $h$ and $\chi$ are smooth.
The function $\chi$ takes values in $[0,1]$, equals zero for $x\leq1$,
and equals one for $x\geq2$.
Moreover, $|\chi'|\leq4$.
To check this bound, write $v=x-1\in(0,1)$.
The derivative is
\[
 \chi'(x)=\frac{h'(v)h(1-v)+h(v)h'(1-v)}{(h(v)+h(1-v))^2}.
\]
Here $0\leq h'(u)\leq4e^{-2}$ for $u>0$, by maximizing
$y^2e^{-y}$.  At least one of $v,1-v$ is at least $1/2$, so
$h(v)+h(1-v)\geq e^{-2}$.  The numerator is at most
$4e^{-2}(h(v)+h(1-v))$, giving the stated bound.
Outside $(1,2)$ the derivative is zero.

For $0<\varepsilon\leq1/[4(k+1)]$, define the smooth cutoff and the
modified polynomial by
\begin{equation}\label{bridge:eq:cutoff}
 \Xi_\varepsilon(t)
 =\chi\left(\frac{1-s}{\varepsilon}\right)
                  \prod_{i=1}^k\chi\left(\frac{t_i}{\varepsilon}\right),
 \qquad F_\varepsilon=\Xi_\varepsilon f.
\end{equation}
The product vanishes unless every coordinate and the slack are at least
$\varepsilon$.  Hence $F_\varepsilon\in\mathcal X_k$.
For a nonzero polynomial $f$, choose constants $C>0$ and $D\geq0$ with
$|f|\leq C$ and $|\partial_i f|\leq D$ on $\Delta_k$, and put
$I=\norm f^2>0$ and $q=R_k(f)$.
For $f=100+us+vp_2+we_2$, valid choices are
\begin{equation}\label{bridge:eq:CD}
 C=100+|u|+|v|+|w|/2,\qquad D=|u|+2|v|+|w|.
\end{equation}
Indeed, $s,p_2\leq1$, $e_2\leq1/2$,
$\partial_i p_2=2t_i$, and $\partial_i e_2=s-t_i$.

\begin{lemma}
The\label{bridge:lem:cutoff} function in \eqref{bridge:eq:cutoff} satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{bridge:eq:cutoff-error}
 \norm{F_\varepsilon-f}^2
 \leq\frac{2(k+1)C^2\varepsilon}{(k-1)!},\qquad
 \norm{F_\varepsilon}_{\mathcal X}
 \leq C+kD+\frac{8kC}{\varepsilon}.
\end{equation}
If $e=\norm{F_\varepsilon-f}/\norm f\leq1/4$, then
\begin{equation}\label{bridge:eq:rayleigh-error}
 \norm{F_\varepsilon}^2\geq(1-e)^2I>0,
 \qquad |R_k(F_\varepsilon)-q|\leq8ke.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The cutoff equals one when all coordinates and the slack are at least
$2\varepsilon$.  For each coordinate, the region
$0\leq t_i<2\varepsilon$ has measure at most
$2\varepsilon/(k-1)!$, by integrating its slices.
The region $0\leq1-s<2\varepsilon$ has measure
\[
 \frac{1-(1-2\varepsilon)^k}{k!}
 \leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{(k-1)!}.
\]
The equality follows by dilating the simplex, and the inequality follows
from $1-(1-z)^k\leq kz$ for $0\leq z\leq1$.
Taking the union of these $k+1$ regions proves the first estimate.
Each derivative of $\Xi_\varepsilon$ has two terms, one from its
coordinate factor and one from its slack factor.
Thus $|\partial_i\Xi_\varepsilon|\leq8/\varepsilon$.
The product rule proves the second estimate.

Write $F=F_\varepsilon$.
The triangle inequality gives $(1-e)\norm f\leq\norm F\leq(1+e)\norm f$.
Since $\norm{K_k}\leq k$,
\[
 |\ip F{K_kF}-\ip f{K_kf}|\leq ke(2+e)I,
 \qquad |\norm F^2-I|\leq e(2+e)I.
\]
Subtract the two Rayleigh quotients and use $0\leq q\leq k$ to obtain
\[
 |R_k(F)-q|\leq\frac{2ke(2+e)}{(1-e)^2}\leq8ke
                         \qquad(0\leq e\leq1/4).
\]
This also proves that $F$ is nonzero.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
Let\label{bridge:prop:smooth} $a$ be a real number with $0\leq a<q$.
Set $g=q-a>0$ and
\begin{equation}\label{bridge:eq:choices}
 \eta=\min\left\{\frac14,\frac{g}{16k}\right\},\qquad
 \varepsilon=\min\left\{\frac1{4(k+1)},
                    \frac{(k-1)!I\eta^2}{2(k+1)C^2}\right\}.
\end{equation}
The width satisfies $0<\varepsilon\leq1/[4(k+1)]$.
Then the explicitly defined $F=F_\varepsilon\in\mathcal X_k$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{bridge:eq:strict-smooth}
 R_k(F)\geq a+\frac g2>a,\qquad
 \norm F^2\geq J:=(1-\eta)^2I,
 \qquad \norm F_{\mathcal X}\leq A:=C+kD+\frac{8kC}{\varepsilon}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $g,I,C$ are positive, both entries defining $\eta$ and both entries
defining $\varepsilon$ are positive.  Thus the cutoff has the stated
positive width and belongs to $\mathcal X_k$.
The first estimate in \eqref{bridge:eq:cutoff-error} gives $e\leq\eta$.
Then \eqref{bridge:eq:rayleigh-error} gives
$R_k(F)\geq q-8k\eta\geq q-g/2=a+g/2$.
The remaining bounds are the other conclusions of
Lemma~\ref{bridge:lem:cutoff}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A conditional counting inequality}

The strict inequality just obtained can pass to finite weighted sums
when their mass and energy errors are controlled.
We specify the required comparison before drawing a counting conclusion.
For positive integers $N$, let $\Omega_N$ be a finite set and let
$\nu_N:\Omega_N\to\{0,1,\ldots,k\}$ be an integer-valued count.
Let $\mathcal V_N:\mathcal X_k\to\mathbb R^{\Omega_N}$ be a linear map,
where the target uses counting measure.
For $G\in\mathcal X_k$, define
\[
 \mathscr S_{1,N}(G)=\sum_{n\in\Omega_N}|(\mathcal V_NG)(n)|^2,
 \qquad
 \mathscr S_{2,N}(G)=\sum_{n\in\Omega_N}\nu_N(n)|(\mathcal V_NG)(n)|^2.
\]
Fix $0<\vartheta\leq1$, an integer $m\geq0$, and one of the nonzero
polynomial trials with $q=R_k(f)>2m/\vartheta$.
Apply Proposition~\ref{bridge:prop:smooth} with $a=2m/\vartheta$.
This fixes $g,\eta,\varepsilon,F,J,A$ before $N$ varies.
Now fix
\begin{equation}\label{bridge:eq:rho-choice}
 \delta=\frac{\vartheta g}{8k},\qquad
 \rho=\frac\vartheta2-\delta,\qquad
 \Gamma=\frac{\vartheta g}{8}>0.
\end{equation}
Since $0<g\leq q\leq k$, one has $0<\delta\leq\vartheta/8<\vartheta/2$.
In particular $\rho>0$.

Assume there are positive numbers $\mathcal C_N$, a positive sequence
$d_N\to\infty$, constants $\alpha_1,\alpha_2>0$, and an integer $N_0$
such that, for $N\geq N_0$ and $G\in\mathcal X_k$,
\begin{align}
 \left|\frac{\mathscr S_{1,N}(G)}{\mathcal C_N}-\norm G^2\right|
 &\leq\frac{\alpha_1}{d_N}\norm G_{\mathcal X}^2,
 \label{bridge:eq:mass-assumption}\\
 \left|\frac{\mathscr S_{2,N}(G)}{\rho\mathcal C_N}-\ip G{K_kG}\right|
 &\leq\frac{\alpha_2}{d_N}\norm G_{\mathcal X}^2.
 \label{bridge:eq:energy-assumption}
\end{align}
These are additional assumptions about the finite maps and counts.

\begin{theorem}
Under\label{bridge:thm:counting} these assumptions, every $N\geq N_0$ with
\begin{equation}\label{bridge:eq:N-condition}
 d_N\geq\max\left\{\frac{2\alpha_1A^2}{J},
                \frac{2(\rho\alpha_2+m\alpha_1)A^2}{\Gamma J}\right\}
\end{equation}
has an element $n\in\Omega_N$ with $\nu_N(n)\geq m+1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $r=R_k(F)$ and $I_F=\norm F^2\geq J$.
Equations~\eqref{bridge:eq:strict-smooth} and~\eqref{bridge:eq:rho-choice}
give
\[
 \rho r-m
 =\frac\vartheta2r-m-\delta r
 \geq\frac{\vartheta g}{4}-\delta k
 =\Gamma.
\]
Here $r\leq k$ follows from the actual operator bound.
The mass comparison and \eqref{bridge:eq:N-condition} give
$\mathscr S_{1,N}(F)\geq\mathcal C_NJ/2>0$.
The two comparisons also give
\begin{align*}
 \mathscr S_{2,N}(F)-m\mathscr S_{1,N}(F)
 &\geq\mathcal C_N\left((\rho r-m)I_F
       -\frac{(\rho\alpha_2+m\alpha_1)A^2}{d_N}\right)\\
 &\geq\frac{\mathcal C_N\Gamma J}{2}>0.
\end{align*}
If every count were at most $m$, the defining nonnegative weights would
instead give $\mathscr S_{2,N}(F)\leq m\mathscr S_{1,N}(F)$.
Thus some count exceeds $m$, and its integer value is at least $m+1$.
\end{proof}

For $m=1$, define $\vartheta_k=2/R_k(f_k)$ from the exact trial values in
Theorem~\ref{var:thm:bounds}.
The numerical condition $\vartheta R_k(f_k)>2$ is precisely
$\vartheta>\vartheta_k$, with
\begin{equation}\label{bridge:eq:theta-table}
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
 k&8&9&10&11&12\\ \hline
 \vartheta_k&\dfrac{90311}{104214}&\dfrac{268142}{319425}&
 \dfrac{143565}{175912}&\dfrac{816072}{1021955}&\dfrac{753}{964}
\end{array}
\end{equation}
Each threshold lies strictly between $1/2$ and $1$.
These finite-dimensional values therefore do not satisfy the $m=1$
condition when $\vartheta\leq1/2$.
At $\vartheta=4/5$, the required quotient is $5/2$.
The exact values give
\[
 R_{11}(f_{11})-\frac52=\frac{1865}{408036}>0,\qquad
 R_{12}(f_{12})-\frac52=\frac{91}{1506}>0.
\]
The comparison polynomials $q_k=K_k^2 1$ instead give
\[
 R_{11}(q_{11})-\frac52=-\frac{973}{12872},\qquad
 R_{12}(q_{12})-\frac52=-\frac{5299}{165262}.
\]
Thus both new trials cross this particular strict threshold.
For example, at $k=12$ and $\vartheta=4/5$ one has
\[
 g=\frac{1928}{753}-\frac52=\frac{91}{1506},\qquad
 \delta=\frac{91}{180720},\qquad
 \rho=\frac{72197}{180720},\qquad
 \Gamma=\frac{91}{15060}.
\]
The formulas above specify a single smooth function $F$ and a sufficient
lower bound on $d_N$ in terms of the assumed comparison constants.
No assertion about particular counts follows without the two comparison
assumptions.

\section{The arithmetic comparison maps}

Write $\mu$ for the M\"obius function: $\mu(n)=0$ when a squared prime
divides $n$, and $\mu(n)=(-1)^r$ when $n$ is a product of $r$ distinct
primes, with $\mu(1)=1$.


The continuum function will determine coefficients through logarithmic
point values.  The transform following those values is finite and invertible.
It is useful to see this transform before imposing an asymptotic regime.
Write $\varphi(q)=|(\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z)^\times|$ for Euler's totient.

\begin{example}
Take\label{ex:mobius} $k=1$, $W=2$, and divisor support $\{1,3,5\}$, corresponding to a
cutoff $R=6$.  The transforms from values $y$ to coefficients $\lambda$
and back are
\[
 L=\begin{pmatrix}1&1/2&1/4\\0&-3/2&0\\0&0&-5/4\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 U=\begin{pmatrix}1&1/3&1/5\\0&-2/3&0\\0&0&-4/5\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad UL=LU=I.
\]
For $y=(1,1,1)$, this gives $\lambda=(7/4,-3/2,-5/4)$.
With shift $0$, $N=10$, and $v_0=1$, the interval contains
$11,13,15,17,19$.  The incidence values are
$7/4,7/4,-1,7/4,7/4$.  Therefore
\[
             S_1=53/4,\qquad S_2=49/4,\qquad S_2/S_1=49/53.
\]
The residue-density expression $(N/W)\sum_r y_r^2/\varphi(r)=35/4$
differs from the exact $S_1$.  Replacing an integer count by its density
requires an error estimate.
\end{example}

For the asymptotic comparison, fix distinct nonnegative shifts
$\mathcal H$, assumed admissible, and a real number $0<\vartheta\leq1$.
The additional analytic hypothesis is that the primes have
\emph{level of distribution $\vartheta$}: for every $A>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lod}
 \sum_{q\leq x^\vartheta}\max_{\gcd(a,q)=1}
 \left|\pi(x;q,a)-\frac{\pi(x)}{\varphi(q)}\right|
       \ll_A\frac{x}{(\log x)^A}.
\end{equation}
Here $\pi(x;q,a)$ counts primes at most $x$ congruent to $a$ modulo $q$,
$\pi(x)$ counts all primes at most $x$.
The implied constant may depend on $A$ and $\vartheta$.
This is the convention in \cite[(1.3), p.~384]{Maynard}.

Fix $0<\delta<\vartheta/2$ and put $\rho=\vartheta/2-\delta$.
For sufficiently large positive integers $N$, set
\[
 D_0=\log\log\log N,\qquad W_N=\prod_{p\leq D_0}p,
 \qquad R_N=N^\rho.
\]
All logarithms are natural.  Choose any admissible class $v_0(N)$
modulo $W_N$, and use it in the definition of $I_N$.
Since the shifts are fixed, $W_N$ contains every prime factor of
$\Delta_{\mathcal H}$ for all sufficiently large $N$.
Also $W_N\leq D_0^{D_0}=N^{o(1)}$, so $I_N$ is then nonempty.
The coefficient space is $\Lambda_N=\mathbb R^{\mathcal D_N}$, where
\[
 \mathcal D_N=\left\{\boldsymbol d\in\mathbb N^k:
 d_1\cdots d_k<R_N,\quad
 \mu^2(d_1\cdots d_k)=1,\quad
 \gcd(d_1\cdots d_k,W_N)=1\right\}.
\]
The set $\mathcal D_N$ is closed under coordinatewise divisors.
Let $Y_N$ be another copy of $\mathbb R^{\mathcal D_N}$, whose coordinates
are denoted by $\boldsymbol r$.

Define linear maps $L_N:Y_N\to\Lambda_N$ and $U_N:\Lambda_N\to Y_N$ by
\begin{align}
 (L_Ny)_{\boldsymbol d}
 &=\left(\prod_i\mu(d_i)d_i\right)
   \sum_{\substack{\boldsymbol r\in\mathcal D_N\\d_i\mid r_i\ \forall i}}
               \frac{y_{\boldsymbol r}}{\prod_i\varphi(r_i)},\label{eq:mobius-L}\\
 (U_N\lambda)_{\boldsymbol r}
 &=\left(\prod_i\mu(r_i)\varphi(r_i)\right)
   \sum_{\substack{\boldsymbol d\in\mathcal D_N\\r_i\mid d_i\ \forall i}}
               \frac{\lambda_{\boldsymbol d}}{\prod_i d_i}.
               \label{eq:mobius-U}
\end{align}
To verify $U_NL_N=I$, substitute the first sum into the second.
For fixed $\boldsymbol r\mid\boldsymbol s$, the intermediate sum is
\[
 \prod_i\sum_{r_i\mid d_i\mid s_i}\mu(d_i)
   =\left(\prod_i\mu(r_i)\right)\mathbf1_{\boldsymbol r=\boldsymbol s}.
\]
Squarefreeness permits $\mu(r_iv)=\mu(r_i)\mu(v)$ here, and
$\sum_{v\mid m}\mu(v)=\mathbf1_{m=1}$ follows by expanding
$\prod_{p\mid m}(1-1)$.  The remaining factors cancel.
Finite-dimensional invertibility then gives $L_NU_N=I$ as well.
These are the transforms in \cite[(5.7)--(5.8), p.~393]{Maynard}.

Use the space $\mathcal X_k$ and its norm defined before
Lemma~\ref{bridge:lem:cutoff}.  This norm controls the point values
needed for sampling.
For $\boldsymbol r\in\mathcal D_N$, put
\[
 x_N(\boldsymbol r)=
 \left(\frac{\log r_1}{\log R_N},\ldots,
       \frac{\log r_k}{\log R_N}\right),\qquad
 (P_NF)_{\boldsymbol r}=F(x_N(\boldsymbol r)).
\]
The coefficient map and the normalized counting-space map are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spine-map}
 E_N=L_NP_N:\mathcal X_k\longrightarrow\Lambda_N,
 \qquad J_N=C_N^{-1/2}T_NE_N:\mathcal X_k\longrightarrow\ell^2(I_N),
\end{equation}
where $\ell^2(I_N)$ uses counting measure, $T_N$ is the incidence map,
and
\[
 C_N=\frac{\varphi(W_N)^kN(\log R_N)^k}{W_N^{k+1}}>0.
\]
Formula~\eqref{eq:mobius-L} makes $E_N$ the exact coefficient map in
\cite[Proposition 4.1, pp.~388--389]{Maynard}.

\section{Uniform comparison of the two forms}

Let $M_{R_{\mathcal H}}$ act on $\ell^2(I_N)$ by multiplication by
$R_{\mathcal H}(n)$.  Define symmetric bilinear forms on $\mathcal X_k$ by
\[
 a_N(F,G)=\langle J_NF,J_NG\rangle,\qquad
 b_N(F,G)=\rho^{-1}
          \langle J_NF,M_{R_{\mathcal H}}J_NG\rangle.
\]
Their diagonal values are the normalized exact sums
\[
 a_N(F,F)=S_1(E_NF)/C_N,\qquad
 b_N(F,F)=S_2(E_NF)/(\rho C_N).
\]
The factor $\rho=\log R_N/\log N$ is fixed before taking a limit.

\begin{theorem}[Comparison on the smooth space]\label{thm:forms}
Assume \eqref{eq:lod} and fix $k,\mathcal H,\vartheta,\delta$ as above.
There are constants $c_1,c_2>0$ and an integer $N_0$, independent of
$F,G\in\mathcal X_k$, such that for $N\geq N_0$,
\begin{align}
 |a_N(F,G)-\langle F,G\rangle|
 &\leq\frac{c_1}{D_0}\|F\|_{\mathcal X}\|G\|_{\mathcal X},
                                                       \label{eq:mass-error}\\
 |b_N(F,G)-\langle F,K_kG\rangle|
 &\leq\frac{c_2}{D_0}\|F\|_{\mathcal X}\|G\|_{\mathcal X}.
                                                       \label{eq:prime-error}
\end{align}
In particular, these forms converge in the norm of bounded bilinear forms
on the normed space $\mathcal X_k$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The arithmetic input is the additive estimate in
\cite[Lemmas 6.2--6.3, pp.~401--404]{Maynard}, with the fixed parameters
specified on pp.~387 and 391.  In the present notation it states
\begin{align*}
 |S_1(E_NF)-C_N\|F\|_2^2|
   &\leq d_1C_N\|F\|_{\mathcal X}^2/D_0,\\
 |S_{2,i}(E_NF)-\rho C_N\|T_iF\|_2^2|
   &\leq d_{2,i}C_N\|F\|_{\mathcal X}^2/D_0.
\end{align*}
Here $S_{2,i}$ uses just the $i$th prime indicator.  The constants and
the lower bound on $N$ are independent of $F$.
The function class used here is contained in the smooth class of those
lemmas.  Their additive estimates apply even when a fibre integral is zero.
Summing the second inequality and dividing by $\rho C_N$ gives the
quadratic estimates for $a_N$ and $b_N$.

For completeness, let $q$ be either error form and suppose
$|q(F,F)|\leq d\|F\|_{\mathcal X}^2/D_0$.
For nonzero $F,G$, put $f=F/\|F\|_{\mathcal X}$ and
$g=G/\|G\|_{\mathcal X}$.  Polarization gives
\[
 |q(f,g)|=\tfrac14|q(f+g,f+g)-q(f-g,f-g)|\leq2d/D_0.
\]
Rescaling proves the stated estimates, with
$c_1=2d_1$ and $c_2=2\rho^{-1}\sum_i d_{2,i}$.
If either function is zero, the result is immediate.
\end{proof}

The arithmetic input is stronger than fixed-function relative asymptotics.
Its constants control all smooth functions through one specified norm.
The place where \eqref{eq:lod} enters is also explicit: the expanded prime
sum counts progressions with moduli
\[
                 q=W_N\prod_i\operatorname{lcm}(d_i,e_i)
                      <W_NR_N^2<N^\vartheta
\]
for sufficiently large $N$.
The strict exponent loss $2\delta$ absorbs $W_N$.
Maynard's estimates (5.19)--(5.20) control the progression errors under
\eqref{eq:lod}.  CRT alone supplies no such estimate.
The constants $d_1,d_{2,i}$ above are the implied constants of the cited
arithmetic estimates.  No numerical values for them or for $N_0$ are
asserted here.

\subsection{From forms to operators}

Let $V\subset\mathcal X_k$ be a nonzero finite-dimensional subspace.
Its $L^2$ geometry controls point values through the finite number
\[
 \beta_V=\sup_{0\ne F\in V}\frac{\|F\|_{\mathcal X}}{\|F\|_2}.
\]
For an $L^2$-orthonormal basis $e_1,\ldots,e_d$ of $V$,
$\beta_V^2\leq\sum_{j=1}^d\|e_j\|_{\mathcal X}^2$ by Cauchy--Schwarz.
Let $P_V:H_k\to V$ be the orthogonal projection, and set
$K_V=P_VK_k|_V$.  Define operators $G_{N,V},H_{N,V}:V\to V$ by
\[
 \langle F,G_{N,V}G\rangle=a_N(F,G),\qquad
 \langle F,H_{N,V}G\rangle=b_N(F,G).
\]
These definitions use the $L^2$ inner product on $V$.

\begin{theorem}[Quantitative finite-dimensional comparison]\label{thm:galerkin}
In the setting of Theorem~\ref{thm:forms}, put
\[
 \varepsilon=\frac{c_1\beta_V^2}{D_0},\qquad
 \eta=\frac{c_2\beta_V^2}{D_0}.
\]
If $N\geq N_0$ and $\varepsilon<1$, then $G_{N,V}$ is positive definite
and both $J_N|_V$ and $E_N|_V$ are injective.
The positive self-adjoint operator
\[
 Q_{N,V}=G_{N,V}^{-1/2}H_{N,V}G_{N,V}^{-1/2}:V\to V
\]
satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spine-bound}
 \|Q_{N,V}-K_V\|
 \leq\frac{\eta+k\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}.
\end{equation}
If $r_V=\max_{0\ne F\in V}\langle F,K_kF\rangle/\|F\|_2^2$, then
\[
 \left|\rho^{-1}
       \max_{0\ne\lambda\in E_N(V)}\frac{S_2(\lambda)}{S_1(\lambda)}
        -r_V\right|
 \leq\frac{\eta+k\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The form bounds give
$\|G_{N,V}-I\|\leq\varepsilon$ and
$\|H_{N,V}-K_V\|\leq\eta$.
Thus $a_N(F,F)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\|F\|_2^2$ on $V$.
This proves positivity and injectivity.

Write $D=G_{N,V}^{-1/2}$ and $s=(1-\varepsilon)^{-1/2}$.
The spectrum of $G_{N,V}$ lies in $[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]$.
Consequently $\|D\|\leq s$ and $\|D-I\|\leq s-1$.
The latter follows because
$1-(1+\varepsilon)^{-1/2}\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{-1/2}-1$.
Now
\[
 Q_{N,V}-K_V=D(H_{N,V}-K_V)D+(D-I)K_VD+K_V(D-I).
\]
Using $\|K_V\|\leq k$ gives
\[
 \|Q_{N,V}-K_V\|
 \leq\eta s^2+k(s-1)(s+1)
 =\frac{\eta+k\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}.
\]
Substituting $F=G_{N,V}^{-1/2}u$ in $b_N(F,F)/a_N(F,F)$
identifies its maximum with $\|Q_{N,V}\|$.
The coefficient map is injective on $V$, so this is exactly the displayed
maximum over $E_N(V)$.  Finally,
$|\|Q_{N,V}\|-\|K_V\||\leq\|Q_{N,V}-K_V\|$.
\end{proof}

This construction also specifies an isometry into the finite counting
space.  Namely,
\[
 Z_{N,V}=J_N|_V\,G_{N,V}^{-1/2}:V\longrightarrow\ell^2(I_N)
\]
satisfies $Z_{N,V}^*Z_{N,V}=I$, and
\[
 Q_{N,V}=\rho^{-1}Z_{N,V}^*M_{R_{\mathcal H}}Z_{N,V}.
\]
The finite operator is therefore a specified compression of prime-count
multiplication.  Estimate~\eqref{eq:spine-bound} compares this compression
with the simplex operator on the same Hilbert space $V$.

\begin{corollary}[A dense sequence of finite comparisons]\label{cor:diagonal}
Let $V_1\subset V_2\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal X_k$ be nonzero
finite-dimensional subspaces with dense union in $H_k$.
Put $\beta_j=\beta_{V_j}$.  Choose integers $j(N)\to\infty$ such that
\[
                    \beta_{j(N)}^2/D_0(N)\longrightarrow0.
\]
For sufficiently large $N$, extend $Q_{N,V_{j(N)}}$ by zero on
$V_{j(N)}^\perp$ to obtain $\widehat K_N:H_k\to H_k$.
Then
\[
 \widehat K_NF\longrightarrow K_kF\quad(F\in H_k),\qquad
 \|\widehat K_N\|\longrightarrow M_k,
\]
and
\[
 \rho^{-1}\max_{0\ne\lambda\in E_N(V_{j(N)})}
              \frac{S_2(\lambda)}{S_1(\lambda)}\longrightarrow M_k.
\]
For $k\geq2$, nevertheless
$\|\widehat K_N-K_k\|\geq M_{k-1}\geq1$ for every $N$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Write $P_j=P_{V_j}$.  The estimate in Theorem~\ref{thm:galerkin} gives
\[
                 \|\widehat K_N-P_{j(N)}K_kP_{j(N)}\|\longrightarrow0.
\]
Nestedness and density imply $P_jF\to F$ for every $F\in H_k$.
Hence
\[
 \|P_jK_kP_jF-K_kF\|
 \leq k\|P_jF-F\|+\|(P_j-I)K_kF\|\longrightarrow0.
\]
The Ritz maxima $r_{V_j}$ are nondecreasing and at most $M_k$.
Given a unit vector with Rayleigh value greater than $M_k-\tau$,
its normalized projections eventually have Rayleigh values greater than
$M_k-2\tau$.  Thus $r_{V_j}\to M_k$.
The same error estimate now proves both limits of maxima.
Corollary~\ref{all:cor:compact-distance} proves the norm lower bound.
Apply $\widehat K_N-K_k$ to a singular sequence at $M_{k-1}$;
the finite-rank term vanishes in norm.

Such sequences $V_j$ exist by the density proved in
Proposition~\ref{prop:simplex} and separability of $L^2$.
Such a diagonal also exists.  For sufficiently large $N$, take
\[
 j(N)=\max\{1\leq j\leq\lfloor D_0(N)\rfloor:
                          \beta_j^2\leq\sqrt{D_0(N)}\}.
\]
Every fixed $j$ eventually satisfies both restrictions.
Thus $j(N)\to\infty$, and the form errors are bounded by constants
times $D_0^{-1/2}$ along this choice.
\end{proof}

\section{Limits of the comparison}

The regularity restriction has an exact finite obstruction.
For any fixed $N$, the sampling nodes $x_N(\mathcal D_N)$ form a finite
set.  Choose a ball in $\Delta_k^\circ$ avoiding that set, and a nonzero
smooth function supported in the ball.  After normalization it gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sampling-obstruction}
 \|F_N\|_2=1,\qquad P_NF_N=E_NF_N=J_NF_N=0,
 \qquad a_N(F_N,F_N)=0.
\end{equation}
Thus the mass forms cannot converge uniformly on the smooth $L^2$ unit
sphere.  Point values also depend on representatives of $L^2$ classes.
They do not define a map on $H_k$.  At an interior sampling node,
shrinking smooth bumps with fixed value demonstrate unbounded point
evaluation in the $L^2$ norm.  For sufficiently large $N$, such a node
exists: apply \eqref{eq:mass-error} to one fixed nonzero interior function.
The resulting positive mass form forces a nonzero sample in the interior.

For comparison, on $\ell^2(\mathbb N)$ let
$C_ne_j=\mathbf1_{j=n}e_j$.  Then $C_nx\to0$ for every $x$, whereas
$\|C_n\|=1$.  Strong convergence by itself does not control maximal
Rayleigh values.  Corollary~\ref{cor:diagonal} obtains that control from
\eqref{eq:spine-bound} and the dense compressions.
Likewise, $De_j=(1-1/j)e_j$ has norm one without a norm eigenvector.
Its compression to $\operatorname{span}(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$ has maximum
$1-1/n$.  Convergence of finite maxima therefore does not prove attainment.
For each finite $k$, Theorem~\ref{all:thm:all-dimensions} supplies
the additional spectral bound and strict dimension inequality that prove attainment.

\subsection{The unrestricted coefficient problem}

Put
\[
 \mathcal R_N=\rho^{-1}
       \max_{\lambda\in\Lambda_N\setminus\ker T_N}
                           \frac{S_2(\lambda)}{S_1(\lambda)}.
\]
The finite principle and Corollary~\ref{cor:diagonal} imply
\[
              M_k\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathcal R_N
                    \leq\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathcal R_N\leq k/\rho.
\]
The upper bound $M_k$ for the last limit requires further control on
all coefficient directions.  The following criterion states that control
on the quotient where the mass form is a norm.

\begin{proposition}[A sufficient condition for the full maximum]
Let\label{prop:full-criterion} $\mathcal Q_N=\Lambda_N/\ker T_N$, with norm
$\|[\lambda]\|_N^2=S_1(\lambda)/C_N$.
Suppose there are linear maps $R_N^\sharp:\mathcal Q_N\to H_k$ and
numbers $u_N,v_N\geq0$ tending to zero such that, for every $z\in\mathcal Q_N$,
\begin{align*}
 \|R_N^\sharp z\|_2^2&\leq(1+u_N)\|z\|_N^2,\\
 \frac{S_2(\lambda)}{\rho C_N}
    &\leq\langle R_N^\sharp z,K_kR_N^\sharp z\rangle
                       +v_N\|z\|_N^2\quad(z=[\lambda]).
\end{align*}
Then $\mathcal R_N\to M_k$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Both $S_1$ and $S_2$ depend only on the class modulo $\ker T_N$.
For $\|z\|_N=1$, the two assumptions give
$S_2(\lambda)/(\rho C_N)\leq M_k(1+u_N)+v_N$.
Take the maximum and combine this upper bound with the preceding liminf.
\end{proof}

The maps and uniform inequalities in this proposition are additional
hypotheses.  Theorem~\ref{thm:forms} verifies the needed comparison only
on the specified smooth families.
It does not construct $R_N^\sharp$ on the entire quotient.
Theorem~\ref{acfr:full-one} supplies such a reconstruction when $k=1$.
For $k\geq2$, control of the full quotient remains a separate requirement.
The positive maximizer of $K_k$ also supplies no such map.
Its $L^2$ construction in Theorem~\ref{all:thm:iteration} does not define point
values for an arbitrary equivalence class or a coefficient reconstruction.

\subsection{A strict prime threshold}

The limiting comparison has a direct consequence with fixed quantifiers.
Assume \eqref{eq:lod}, and let $m$ be a nonnegative integer.
If $M_k>2m/\vartheta$, choose first $F\in\mathcal X_k$ with
\[
 r(F)=\langle F,K_kF\rangle/\|F\|_2^2>2m/\vartheta.
\]
Then choose a fixed $0<\delta<\vartheta/2$ small enough that
$(\vartheta/2-\delta)r(F)>m$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:forms} gives
\[
                 \frac{S_2(E_NF)}{S_1(E_NF)}\longrightarrow\rho r(F)>m.
\]
For every sufficiently large $N$, Proposition~\ref{prop:finite} supplies
an integer $n\in[N,2N)$ with at least $m+1$ prime translates.
Taking disjoint intervals proves that there are infinitely many such integers.
This is the strict threshold in \cite[Proposition 4.2, pp.~389--390]{Maynard}.
Bombieri--Vinogradov supplies \eqref{eq:lod} for every
$\vartheta<1/2$ in this convention.  Consequently $M_k>4m$ suffices,
by choosing $2m/M_k<\vartheta<1/2$ before fixing $F$ and $\delta$.


\subsection{The five polynomial thresholds}

The polynomial bounds give concrete sufficient distribution exponents.
The cutoff in Proposition~\ref{bridge:prop:smooth} supplies a function
in the actual smooth space used by the coefficient map $E_N$.

\begin{corollary}
Fix\label{bridge:cor:prime} $k\in\{8,9,10,11,12\}$ and a fixed admissible set
$\mathcal H$ of $k$ distinct nonnegative shifts.
Let $0<\vartheta\leq1$ satisfy the distribution hypothesis
\eqref{eq:lod} and the strict inequality
\[
                         \vartheta>\vartheta_k,
\]
where $\vartheta_k$ is the corresponding fraction in
\eqref{bridge:eq:theta-table}.
Then infinitely many integers $n$ have at least two primes among
$n+h_1,\ldots,n+h_k$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Take the explicit polynomial $f_k$ in \eqref{var:eq:trials} and put
$q=R_k(f_k)$, $m=1$, and $g=q-2/\vartheta>0$.
Choose $C,D$ by \eqref{bridge:eq:CD}, and use its exact mass
$I=D_k/(k+4)!$ from \eqref{var:eq:scaled}.
Equations~\eqref{bridge:eq:choices} and~\eqref{bridge:eq:cutoff}
then specify a width $0<\varepsilon\leq1/[4(k+1)]$ and a single
nonzero $F_\varepsilon\in\mathcal X_k$.  Put $F=F_\varepsilon$.
Fix $\delta,\rho,\Gamma$ by \eqref{bridge:eq:rho-choice}.
All these choices are made before $N$ varies.

For the finite counting construction in Theorem~\ref{bridge:thm:counting},
use
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 \Omega_N=I_N,\qquad \nu_N(n)=R_{\mathcal H}(n),\qquad
 \mathcal V_N=T_NE_N,\\
 \mathcal C_N=C_N,\qquad d_N=D_0=\log\log\log N.
 \end{gathered}
\]
Here $T_N$ is the divisor-incidence map for $\mathcal D_N$ and $I_N$.
Its squared values are nonnegative weights, and $\nu_N$ takes integer
values between zero and $k$.
The finite sums become exactly
\[
 \mathscr S_{1,N}(F)=S_1(E_NF),\qquad
 \mathscr S_{2,N}(F)=S_2(E_NF).
\]
Under \eqref{eq:lod}, Theorem~\ref{thm:forms} gives
\eqref{bridge:eq:mass-assumption} and~\eqref{bridge:eq:energy-assumption}
with $\alpha_1=c_1$ and $\alpha_2=c_2$.
Since $D_0\to\infty$, the explicit condition
\eqref{bridge:eq:N-condition} holds for every sufficiently large $N$.
Theorem~\ref{bridge:thm:counting} then gives an $n\in I_N$ with
$R_{\mathcal H}(n)\geq2$.
Apply this conclusion on disjoint intervals $[N,2N)$ to obtain infinitely
many distinct integers.
\end{proof}

For example, both $k=11$ and $k=12$ permit the conditional choice
$\vartheta=4/5$.  The explicit cutoff and exponent loss for $k=12$
were computed above.
This example assumes \eqref{eq:lod} at $4/5$.
The constants $c_1,c_2$ and $N_0$ retain their meanings from
Theorem~\ref{thm:forms}; no numerical values for them are supplied by the
polynomial calculation.
All five fractions $\vartheta_k$ exceed $1/2$, so these trials do not reach
the strict two-prime criterion using only exponents $\vartheta<1/2$.

The comparison identifies a controlled family of finite prime-count
compressions with the simplex operator in the strong topology.
It also determines the limiting maxima on that family without assuming an
attaining eigenfunction.  A full-coefficient upper bound requires the
uniform reconstruction in Proposition~\ref{prop:full-criterion}.
The intrinsic spectral argument determines the essential interval and
attainment for every finite $k$.  These conclusions remain separate from the
arithmetic reconstruction on the full coefficient quotient.
